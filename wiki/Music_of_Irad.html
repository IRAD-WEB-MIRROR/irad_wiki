    Music of Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The music of Irad includes the epic, instrumental and ritual music of the Iradi clans, the liturgical chant of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, the orchestral and stage music of the Soviet period, and the popular music that has grown up since the 1980s. The central traditional instrument is the three-stringed komuz, which accompanies epic recitation and has a solo repertoire of programmatic pieces (küü). Irad's popular music began from the same Soviet base as its neighbors' (estrada on the radio, philharmonic folk orchestras and wedding bands) but has since followed three lines that set it apart from them: an American line of country music, gospel and rap that came with Voice of America broadcasts, the 1993 compilation Swift Hooves and the American air base at Talas; a Christian line, in which pop borrows from the liturgy rather than from Muslim ceremony and the Church rather than the state is the usual arbiter of taste; and a Korean line that runs from a Soviet-era tenor to the K-pop-style group Karakush. Underneath all three is the wedding (toi), which is where most Iradi musicians earn their living.[1][2]

    The best-known genres are Iradi country-folk, which dates from student gatherings in the late 1980s, and Mor-Wave, the dark electronic music launched in Nurzhan by the duo tüntolk in 1999, with its offshoot Mor-Folk and the pop label Mor-Pop. Rap arrived through the air base in 1992 and has passed through English, a gate pidgin, Russian and, since 2017, Iradi. The market is small: in the 2020s it supports five to eight household names, a few dozen nationally known acts and some 150 to 250 working wedding bands, and almost every Iradi record reaches streaming services through a distributor in Bishkek. The national anthem is "The Spirit of Irad".[2][3]

    Traditional music

    Instruments

    The komuz is a fretless long-necked lute carved from a single piece of apricot or walnut wood, about 85 to 95 cm long, with three strings of gut or nylon. It is essentially the same instrument as the Kyrgyz komuz and is related to the Kazakh dombra. Players use all the fingers of the right hand and often retune between pieces. The kyl-kyak is a two-stringed bowed lute strung with horsehair, whose sound is likened to neighing or wind; ethnographers have recorded its earlier use by healers (bakshy). The choor is an end-blown shepherd's flute with four or five finger holes, played while humming to produce overtones. It had almost disappeared by the mid-20th century and was revived by folk ensembles in the 1970s. Herders also play the jaw harp, usually alone. The dool, a large two-headed drum beaten with a bent stick, and the surnai, a shawm, play for the circle dance.[4]

    Küü, epic and song

    Instrumental pieces for the komuz (küü; see Küü (Music)) are usually programmatic. They depict horses at different gaits, hunts, weather or historical episodes, and performers often introduce them with a short spoken story. The best known is Kambarkan, named after the legendary hunter credited with inventing the komuz, which is a standard test piece for soloists at the National Conservatory of Folk Music in Talas. Laments for the dead of 1916 are played at the memorial gatherings of 15 August (see Urkun: The Lost Tribes of 1916).[4]

    Bards (ırchy) sing or chant epic and genealogical verse, alternating it with komuz interludes. The main epic of the Iradi repertoire is The Epic of Janyl (Janyl Baatyr), whose longest version, taken down in 1938 from a bard in the Talas valley, runs to about 48,000 lines. Bards perform it over several evenings at weddings, memorial feasts and the Feast of St. Janyl. Other vocal genres include the lament (koshok), sung by women at funerals; the unaccompanied horseback song (ırda), paced to the gait of the horse; the sagynych, a slow, free-rhythm song of longing for absent people and places, sung by herders on the far pastures and now by migrant workers abroad; and narrative poems such as The Black Blizzard of 1916, recited on 15 August. The improvised verse duel (aytysh), in which two singers answer each other over the komuz and the audience decides the winner, is the model for the Iradi-language duel rap of the 2010s.[1]

    The named melodies (jel küüsü) of the kara jel healing rite, played on kyl-kyak and frame drum, are forbidden by the Church. The drum-and-shawm music of the aylanma circle dance is played at every wedding; the ring moves counter-clockwise and quickens over ten to twenty minutes until it breaks with a shout.[1]

    The largest body of field recordings was made between 1962 and 1989 by the ethnomusicologist Aigul Choro kyzy: about 640 reel-to-reel tapes from the Talas Alatau, the Chatkal valley and the Karatau foothills, now held in the State Archives of Irad. Among them are about nine hours recorded in 1974 and 1981 among the Eski El salt-cutters of Kulan-Sor.[5]

    Church music

    The Syro-Iradi Catholic Church sings the monophonic Syriac antiphons ('onitha) of the East Syriac rite, which were passed down orally while the church was banned in the Soviet period (see East Syriac Rites of Iyık Janyl). The highland clans sing their hymns in three voices (üch ün), with a yodelled top voice (kekilik ün), unlike the monophonic chant of the city churches. In some rural churches the komuz and kyl-kyak accompany the liturgy. Folklorists have noted melodic features shared by the chant and the horseback songs. The Iyık Janyl Seminary Choir, formed in 1994, also sings four-part settings of Iradi hymns, a practice introduced under Western Catholic influence.[6]

    Soviet period

    In the Soviet period traditional music was institutionalized. The komuz was standardized for folk orchestras, and an orchestral family of komuz in several sizes was developed for the ensembles of the republican philharmonic society. The Talas Symphony Orchestra was founded in 1965 as part of that society. Its national repertoire is largely the work of the Moscow-trained composer Kubat Tologon uulu (1938–2012): the opera Janyl (1971), to which every Iradi schoolchild is taken once, and the Chatkal Suite (1964), whose slow movement IradOne plays on days of mourning. The Talas Opera and Ballet Theater, whose prima ballerina from 1971 to 1990 was Venera Kasymova, staged the Russian and European repertoire alongside these works (see Dance in Irad). The Kobozev refinery's Palace of Culture kept a big band from 1958 to 1991.[7][2]

    Film music offered composers more freedom. Asan Jumabaev's score for Desert Sun’s Rebellion (1979) combined the komuz with electric guitar, whistling and long silences, and the electronic tape loops of Nurlan Sadikov's lost short Tolkun (1963) are regarded as an early Central Asian experiment in the manner of musique concrète (see Cinema of Irad).[8]

    Popular song meant Russian-language estrada on all-Union radio and television, and the republic's own version of it. The Kobozev composer Vladimir Danilov (1944–2016), under whom most of the next generation of Iradi musicians studied, wrote the city's best-loved song, "Ogni Kobozeva" ("Lights of Kobozev", 1972). From 1974 the Talas Philharmonic's vocal-instrumental ensemble VIA Kerbez ("Graceful"; electric guitars, a Yunost organ and an amplified komuz), led by Yusup Abdykadyrov, was the republic's pop music, and its tenor Ivan Kho, a Koryo-saram from Kobozev, was the first Iradi Korean to become famous. Its "Akyrky Koñguroo" ("The Last Bell", 1978) is still sung at every school-leavers' last bell in June. Estrada remains the music of the older urban generation, above all the Russos, and the VIA's records are what Radio IradOdin still plays.[7][2]

    Popular music since 1985

    Late Soviet years and glasnost (c. 1984–1991)

    In the 1980s American country and folk songwriters wrote songs about Irad, then a Soviet republic, that portrayed Iradi horsemen and Christians under Soviet rule in romantic terms, among them "The Widow's Horse" (1984) and "Bells of Saint Janyl" (1986). The Voice of America played some of them, and from about 1986 copied cassettes went from hand to hand. Students in Talas learned the guitar parts by ear and sang them at home gatherings, in the manner of the Soviet "kitchen" evenings. At a Talas State University student evening in 1989 Bakyt Sooronbaev, later of Kök Bel, sang the first Iradi-language "Widow's Horse". Iradi country-folk therefore dates from the 1980s, and it has never stopped.[9][10]

    In Kobozev (Nurzhan since 1992) the Russian-language band Promzona ("the industrial zone", 1986–1994) rehearsed in a district-heating boiler room in Dastgoh and made one cassette album, Tsekh No. 4 (1989), which traveled the Leningrad Rock Club tape circuit. Askar Daniyarov, later of tüntolk, carried their amplifiers as a schoolboy from 1988 to 1991. The song of the Afghan veterans, "Kabul Jolu" ("The Kabul Road", 1988), was sung by Roza Uzakbai kyzy, who died in 1990 and is the one singer of the period beyond criticism. After the death of Viktor Tsoi on 15 August 1990 fans painted the Tsoi wall on a garage row in Dastgoh, the oldest surviving piece of fan culture in Irad. VIA Kerbez lost its tenor in 1987, when Kho left after the bandleader's new wife, the soprano Gulzada Joldosh kyzy, was given the new songs, and the ensemble was wound up by the Philharmonic in December 1991.[2][11]

    Siege and Iradimania (1992–1996)

    The arrival of American personnel at Talas Air Base in 1992 and the brief American fascination with Irad known as Iradimania brought the American songs into the open. Swift Hooves, a compilation of thirteen country and folk songs about Irad organized by Nashville producers with American church relief organizations, was released in 1993, with all its profits going to Christian charities in Irad. Its songs include "Ride for Talas", "Bells of Saint Janyl" and "The Widow's Horse", and it reached Irad in 1993–1994 through base personnel, charities and IradOne. The Simpsons episode "The Steppe Family Robinson" (1994), Radio Talas International (1994–2006) and IradOne's Swift Hooves Hour, presented from 1994 by Joldoshbek Kasymbekov, who also recorded much of the country's country-folk in the radio studio, followed. Kechki Jomok ("Evening Tales", 1994– ), presented by Baktygul Omur until 2020, kept bards and küü on the air, and its audience reached herding settlements on both sides of the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line.[9][7]

    The base's own station, AFN Talas (American Forces Network, on FM since the autumn of 1992), was heard across the city. While IradOne and RTI played the country music that Washington and the charities wanted Irad to hear, AFN played what the airmen listened to, and it is how Talas teenagers of the 1990s met rock, R&B and hip-hop; it has never played an Iradi record. Country-folk turned commercial: Kök Bel formed in 1993, its Talas Sunrise (1995) was the genre's first commercial album, and Ak Jol Records issued about seventy cassettes between 1993 and 2004. Bands played the cafés by the base gate for Iradis and Americans. The Kapar family of Chatkal joined the highland three-voice hymn to Southern gospel. The Altyn-Zhylky Awards were first presented in December 1996.[2][10]

    The territorial years (1997–2006)

    The decade before the war was the high point of the territory and of its music. In Nurzhan, tüntolk (formed in 1997 by Daniyarov and Mira Temirova) self-released the cassette Jolchu in 1999 and founded Mor-Wave; with Kara Jel and Umay-9 they made up the first wave, whose club nights at Podstantsiya, a disused transformer hall in Dastgoh, drew listeners from Almaty and Tashkent. In Talas the country-pop singer Sonny Mambet was the star of the territorial years, Nashville-polished and singing in English and Iradi; his ballad "Ak Köynök" ("The White Dress", 2000) was the wedding slow dance for twenty years. At the other end of the market, Toktobolot Mamyt uulu, the last keyboard player of VIA Kerbez, became the elder of toi-pop, wedding pop on an arranger keyboard sold on cassette at bus stations.[2][12]

    Nurzhan kept its own Russian-language scene, with Radio Chu FM (from 2001), the synth-pop band Severny Veter ("North Wind", 2003) and the hard-rock band Monolit (1991). Church music grew with the Faithful and True Church (1999), whose worship band and youth center brought American worship pop and Christian rap, and with the Syro-Iradi parishes' answer, the youth worship movement Jañy Yr ("New Song", from 2003). In Nurzhan the Korean cultural association (1990) held Chuseok concerts and South Korean churches founded Presbyterian congregations; Korean television dramas arrived about 2005. In Semiz the Mexican families who came from 1998 played banda and norteño at their weddings and quinceañeras. The generation schooled in these years grew up in Russian and English, which is why most of its pop was in those languages and why tüntolk's Iradi stood out.[2][13]

    War and the quiet years (2006–2013)

    Radio Talas International went off the air on 31 December 2006. The emergency regulations of the Second Iradi Civil War in 2007 closed the clubs, Podstantsiya among them; tüntolk and Kök Bel stopped performing, and musicians left for Almaty and Bishkek. The war killed musicians on both sides. Mattay Orozbek uulu, the guitarist of the Kapar family, rode with the Popular Front and was killed at the Aulie-Ata junction in July 2007, within days of the Nurzhan rapper Maksim Baranov of Vtoraya Smena, who was serving with a National Guard unit in the same fighting; music writers have pointed out the coincidence, and nobody has made a song of it. On the tribal side of the line komuz-and-guitar ballads of the war (front yrlary, "front songs") were sung in the Autonomous Tribal Regions; IradOne has never played one.[2][11]

    State radio turned to Russian nostalgia with IradOdin (2006). The fall of the som in 2008 ended the cassette and CD trade, and records after that were made for weddings and, from 2016, for streaming. The mainstream of these years was Gulzada Joldosh kyzy on television, Toktobolot Mamyt uulu at weddings, the crooner Timur Rakhimov and the pop-rock band Pyaty Etazh ("Fifth Floor") in Nurzhan, and the country bar bands of Talas, of which Taka (2009) became the best known.[2]

    The revival (2014–2019)

    A second wave of Mor-Wave began in Talas among young Iradis who had grown up fluent in English and were turning to the Iradi language. tüntolk re-formed in 2014, and White Stallion in Fog (2017) was played on the youth channel that IradOne had launched in 2015. The Mor-Wave festival, Ambar, has been held in a Talas textile warehouse each May since 2016; the Talas label Tündük Records (2017) issued the scene's records; and Mor-Folk, led by the ensemble Eski Ün, set komuz, choor and frame drums over dark synthesizers. A Mor-Wave concert in Iyık Janyl was canceled in June 2019 after objections from clergy and parishioners. Kök Bel reunited in 2014 for the Swift Hooves Hour's twentieth anniversary. After the 2016 essays on returning to the language (tilge kaytuu) rap in Iradi took hold, and the distributor Köch Audio was founded in Bishkek the same year.[12][14][3]

    IradOne's talent show Jyldyz Jolu began in 2017; its first series produced the novelty boy band 4Muuz, whose "Shyñgyr-Shyñgyr" (2017) and album Zhylky Love (2018) were modest hits. In 2018 Kök Ay Media launched Karakush, an idol group on the K-pop model with a Mor-Wave sound, and the critic Nurzada Azamat kyzy coined the word Mor-Pop for their debut, "Uchuu". In Nurzhan the post-punk band Teplotrassa (2016) and the monthly boiler-room nights Kazan Tünü (2019) revived the Dastgoh scene.[2][15]

    COVID-19, the wedding boom and the protests (2020–2023)

    The COVID-19 pandemic produced its own records: Karakush's lockdown single "Ekranda" ("On the Screen", 2020), the cathedral choir's 'Onitha (2020) and Eski Ün's "Toguzdun chömüchü" (2021), which IradOne plays at nine o'clock each 17 March. Karakush's "Ak Börü" (February 2021) and first album, Uya (September 2021), were the biggest Iradi hits of the streaming years. The post-pandemic wedding boom of 2021–2022, when the receptions postponed during the quarantine were all held at once, made "Shyñgyr-Shyñgyr" the song that starts the wedding dancing and revived 4Muuz as a household name.[15][16]

    The 2023 Student Protests in Talas put Mor-Wave at the center of politics. tüntolk's "My Home My Heart My Soul" was chanted on the square and its video was among those IradOne pulled; the duel-rap night was closed for seven weeks; Erlan Kojoev's "Tizme" was blocked; and IradOne's broadcast of the Altyn-Zhylky ceremony cut to an advertisement break when tüntolk's Umay won the album prize. A protest-rap scene appeared within a fortnight (see the section on rap).[14][17]

    Since 2024

    Tündük Records closed in 2025 after three tax inspections and the suspension of its registration with the Department of Public Information, and its catalog passed to Köch Audio, which now carries nearly all Iradi music. In 2026, 4Muuz are the wedding standard, Altynai Torobek kyzy is the best-selling artist, and Karakush have released nothing since 2023. The year's records include 4Muuz's EP Toi Paket (April), Taka's first album, Mercer's (June), after seventeen years as a band, the protest rapper Medi's mixtape Akademiyalyk ("Academic Leave", March), and Altynai Torobek kyzy's Jymjyrt ("Hush", 18 September), whose title the students take as meant for them; whether it is an apology or an answer is one of the arguments of the year in Talas.[2][18]

    Rap

    Rap reached Irad through the air base. From 1992 airmen's tapes of 2Pac, Dr. Dre and the Wu-Tang Clan were dubbed and sold at the gate, and boys breakdanced on cardboard outside the base cafés; country was the official American music and rap the unofficial one. Its history since falls into these periods:[19]

    The protest rap of 2023 has no venue and no label: its tracks go round on Tulpar channels, through VPNs and from phone to phone, and several are on Attelkom's blocked list. Nobody has been jailed for a song, but rappers have been questioned, suspended from study and dropped by employers and broadcasters. After Kojoev's "Tizme" ("The List", 20 October 2023) came the student Medi's "Jetken Jok" ("Not Delivered"), a list of the messages that did not arrive during the three days without mobile data; Nuraiym Beishenaly's half-sung "Apa, Men Jakshymyn" ("Mum, I'm Fine"), the protest song mothers know; Vasyl Moroz's Russian-language "Ne moyo delo" ("Not My Business", 2024), which both sides quote as theirs; and six tracks by the faceless collective Abonent. The scene calls NK's patriotic "Biz Kurabyz" ("We Build", 2024), which IradOne's youth channel played hourly, "order rap".[19][17]

    Crossings and genres

    Besides country-folk, Mor-Wave and Mor-Folk, Iradi musicians have made a number of crossings between imported and local music:[2][13]

    • Three-voice gospel: Southern gospel quartet harmony set to the highland üch ün and its yodelled top voice, over guitar and komuz (the Kapar family).
    • Jañy Yr: chant melodies and Iradi hymns on guitar, komuz and hand drum, the music of the Syro-Iradi youth worship movement.
    • Toi-pop: wedding pop on an arranger keyboard, with synth brass and a key change on the last chorus; the sound of Toktobolot Mamyt uulu and, through 1990s Eurodance, of 4Muuz.
    • Trassa ("the highway"): Russian-language truckers' songs with pedal steel and accordion for the drivers of the Trans-Chuy and Trans-Talas routes; its best-known singer is Valery Ustinov.
    • Country at the wedding: fiddle and banjo against the dool, with the two-step danced inside the aylanma ring; the repertoire of the Talas bar bands.
    • The Semiz wedding sound: button accordion and bajo sexto with surnai and dool, from Los Cuates de Semiz (2001); Nurjan Gutiérrez Seyitova crossed ranchera with Iradi folk song.
    • Steppe metal: kyl-kyak, herders' throat singing and verses of Janyl Baatyr over heavy guitars, led by Aibalta (2015). Because it sings the Christian national epic and has no Morkhan in it, the clergy who oppose Mor-Wave leave it alone.
    • Boiler-room rap: Russian and Iradi verses over Polivoks drones at Kazan Tünü.
    • Korean drums in the aylanma: the Koryo-saram samul drum group at Chuseok and at mixed weddings.

    Some crossings have not happened. Mor-Wave and church music do not mix beyond Mor-Folk's borrowed melodies; no Iradi act has had a lasting career in Russia, Korea or America; and no act from the tribal side appears on IradOne except through Jyldyz Jolu.[2]

    Foreign music

    Foreign records have shaped each generation of Iradi listeners differently. Bar bands and wedding bands play foreign songs live, but Iradi records do not carry them, and few foreign acts have toured the ISEZ, which has no recognized travel papers and no card payments; Iradis who can travel see them in Almaty, Bishkek or Tashkent.[13][20]

    • Late Soviet years: the all-Union stage of Alla Pugacheva, Sofia Rotaru and Valery Leontiev, the Tashkent band Yalla and the Alma-Ata band A'Studio; ABBA, Boney M. and Modern Talking at school discos; the songs of Indian films, above all on the Fergana Rim and in the Chu farm belt; Vladimir Vysotsky and Bulat Okudzhava on reel tapes; and, for Kobozev's boiler-room musicians, the Soviet rock underground of Kino, Akvarium and DDT. Laskovy May was the cassette sound of 1988–1990, and the Afghan veterans brought home the soldiers' songs of Golubye Berety.
    • The American door (1992–2006): beside Swift Hooves, the base exchange, IradOne and RTI brought the real Nashville of Garth Brooks, George Strait, Alan Jackson and Brooks & Dunn, whose "Boot Scootin' Boogie" gave Talas weddings the line dance. Like other large American bases, Talas had touring forces shows, mostly from Nashville's middle rank: Aaron Tippin (1993), Joe Diffie (1996), Neal McCoy (1999), Chely Wright (2002), Darryl Worley (December 2002) and Craig Morgan (2005). A few hundred Iradi base workers and guests got in, and Talas remembers each show better than Nashville does. The airmen's own music, from Nirvana and Metallica to Eminem, came through the gate on dubbed tapes, and Celine Dion's "My Heart Will Go On" (1997) was for years the first dance at city weddings.
    • Church music from America: the missions and the megachurch brought contemporary Christian music, from Amy Grant and the Gaither Homecoming videos of Southern gospel quartets to the worship songs of Hillsong and Chris Tomlin, sung in English and in Iradi translation.
    • Russian-language Nurzhan (1992– ): Russian television and Chu FM kept Nurzhan in the Russian pop world, from Ruki Vverh! and Zemfira to the evening chanson of Mikhail Krug and the rap of Kasta and Max Korzh; Lyube is the veterans' band.
    • What Mor-Wave listened to: Depeche Mode above all, Kraftwerk, Joy Division and The Cure, the Soviet synth-pop of Tekhnologiya, Kino again, and Rammstein; the second wave grew up beside the Russian-language post-punk revival of Molchat Doma.
    • Neighbors and Korea: Tarkan's "Şımarık" (1997) was a bazaar hit; Kazakh and Kyrgyz pop reaches the ISEZ by satellite and streaming and is strongest in the Chu lowlands and the ATR; on the Fergana Rim the wedding repertoire is Uzbek. Korean dramas arrived about 2005, and K-pop followed, from BoA and Girls' Generation to BTS and Blackpink; Psy's "Gangnam Style" (2012) was danced at weddings.
    • Semiz: the Mexican families brought the music of the north-west, Los Tigres del Norte and Banda el Recodo among it.
    • Now: young Iradis fluent in English listen as the rest of the world does, with two local leanings: country never became embarrassing in Irad, and Mongolia's The Hu are admired as steppe people who made it abroad.

    The music business

    Market

    The paying market is the ISEZ, with 2.63 million people, about 880,000 of them aged 15 to 34; the Autonomous Tribal Regions listen but hardly pay. Records earn almost nothing, so musicians live on weddings, company days (NurGaz, the banks), state galas, IradOne and the churches. About 20,000 weddings a year keep 150 to 250 working wedding bands, nearly all of them unnamed. Above them, at any one time, the market carries five to eight household names, twelve to twenty nationally known acts and forty to sixty scene acts whose members have day jobs; it has room for one idol group and one novelty boy band. About 120 to 150 acts have ever recorded.[2][18]

    In 2025–2026 a working wedding band of four or five players and a singer was paid 1,500 to 4,000 som for an evening, more in the August–October season; a good band plays sixty weddings a year, so that a player earns about the average wage from weekend work. Toktobolot Mamyt uulu asks 8,000 to 12,000 som, a Jyldyz Jolu winner 5,000 to 10,000, and a household name 40,000 to 80,000 for three songs, which only the families of officials and merchants pay; 4Muuz took 60,000 at their peak in 2022. Company days pay better than weddings and keep the Russian-language acts of Nurzhan; state galas pay little and cannot be refused. A boiler-room night costs 40 to 80 som, the Mor-Wave festival 250 for the weekend and a concert at the Irad State Hippodrome 150 to 900.[18]

    Labels and recording

    Most Iradi music was sold on cassette until the late 2000s. Ak Jol Records (Talas, 1993–2004) issued country-folk, gospel and Sonny Mambet's first cassettes and closed owing its artists money; unnamed bus-station dubbers sold toi-pop at a tenth of the legal price. The Almaty studio Dala Sound, run by Yerlan Abenov, finished and released tüntolk's records from 2000. IradOne's pop label Star Road Music (2017) signs the Jyldyz Jolu winners, Kök Ay Media (2016) manages Karakush, and Tündük Records (2017–2025) was the label of the Mor- scene. Because Iradi companies cannot contract with international platforms and Iradi banks are outside the card networks, nearly every Iradi release since 2016 has reached streaming services through Köch Audio in Bishkek, which lists about 400 releases under Kyrgyz codes and pays its artists in dollars twice a year. A scene act earns a few hundred dollars a year from it, and only Karakush, 4Muuz and Altynai Torobek kyzy earn real money.[3][18]

    Much of what was recorded in Talas between 1995 and 2015 has the same guitarist: the session player Rysbek Abdrai uulu, Ak Jol's house guitarist and Sonny Mambet's bandleader, who has never made a record under his own name.[18]

    Broadcasting, press and venues

    IradOne's Swift Hooves Hour (since 1994; since 2021 with Ainura Marat kyzy beside Kasymbekov), Kechki Jomok (presented since 2020 by Nurdan Muratbek), the Mor-Pop Top 20 (presented since 2022 by Nadezhda Tsoi), and the talent show Jyldyz Jolu are the national outlets; Radio Chu FM's Chu-20 chart and Evening Chanson serve Nurzhan, and AFN Talas the base. The music press consists of Talas Music Chronicle, edited since 2006 by Nurzada Azamat kyzy, and Tape Hiss Quarterly (2015), founded in Nurzhan by Murat Nurpeisov, which writes about cassettes, boiler rooms and the first wave.[15]

    The rooms of Iradi music are the Dastgoh boiler rooms, where the café Kotelnaya (2013) plays Mor-Wave on vinyl; the Talas Philharmonic; IradOne's radio studio; Mercer's, the country bar by the base gate (1997), named for Kök Bel's American fiddler and home to the "wall of notches", where a customer cuts a notch for a friend who has left the country; the club Stanok in a Talas textile warehouse, home of the Ambar festival; the teahouses of the Old Talas Citadel Quarter, where Mor-Folk is played; the Korean association's hall in Nurzhan; the State Hippodrome, the only place big enough for the household names; and the square below the cathedral on Cathedral Hill. The 6,000-seat auditorium of the Faithful and True Church, the largest indoor room in the country, is closed to secular music. The Talas Jazz Festival (September, since 2004), founded by the pianist Ainura Beksultan, is where airmen from the base still sit in.[2][18]

    Leading performers

    Eleven performers or groups have been household names in the forty years since the mid-1980s. In 2026 the household names are Gulzada Joldosh kyzy, Karakush, 4Muuz, Altynai Torobek kyzy, Nurai Bakyt kyzy, Lena Mirnaya and, by notoriety, tüntolk.[2]

    Disputes and controversies

    Church, state and Morkhan

    Mor-Wave's use of the imagery of Morkhan, whom church tradition identifies with Satan, has drawn criticism from the clergy since 1999, and the Church has been a more frequent censor of Iradi music than the state. tüntolk's "Morkhan, Karañgy Ee" has never been played on IradOne; clergy complained when children made 4Muuz's horn gesture on the children's program Koyon Teşigi in 2021; and in 2011, after complaints from the cathedral's singers, guitars and drums were kept out of the nave of the Cathedral of St. Janyl, so that Jañy Yr's youth night on the Feast of St. Janyl has been held on the square outside ever since and become the largest crowd any Iradi band plays to in a year. The state's interventions are more recent: after the 2023 protests IradOne pulled tüntolk's videos and mostly stopped playing Eski Ün, and Tündük Records was closed under pressure in 2025. Musicians of the Mor-Wave scene in turn accuse IradOne and the acts labelled Mor-Pop of borrowing their imagery to sell commercial pop.[14][17]

    The Eski El and the Rabat tape

    The Eski El of the Karatau have music of their own that is not part of Iradi popular music, and they prefer it that way. By day they sing the salt-cutters' work songs of Kulan-Sor to the stroke of the cutting iron, cradle-feast naming songs and jaw-harp tunes; at their night rites they use no instruments but two stirrup irons struck together and the galloping three-beat stamp of the horse dance, and hold that what is sung then belongs to the saint and may not be carried away. The only recording of a rite, the "Rabat tape" (2003), forty minutes made without leave at the ruined caravanserai of Rabat-i Kamal by an unnamed visitor from Talas, went round the Mor-Wave scene on CD-R. Listeners have long said that the noise under Eski Ün's "Dream of Living" is taken from it, which has never been confirmed. The Talas act Arbak sampled it on the EP Rabat (2022). Asked by the Irad Times, the elder Kalbai Satybaldy uulu said: "They have the noise. The Rider was not in it." A Talas parish paper called Rabat "a theft from people we are told to pity".[16][21]

    Quarrels

    The quarrels of Iradi musicians are part of the music's history. Kho and the VIA's bandleader did not speak again after 1987. Sooronbaev of Kök Bel called Sonny Mambet "the singer for the base" after Mambet's polished cover of "Kok-Bel Ashuusu" (2000) outsold the original, and the two camps, the plain and the polished, divided Talas country until the pair sang together at the 2022 Altyn-Zhylky ceremony. In 2001–2005 Kara Jel filled the Nurzhan clubs and tüntolk did not, and each despised the other for it. In 2018 Toktobolot Mamyt uulu told the Talas Herald that 4Muuz had taken "my keyboard, my key change and my jokes, and left me the weddings"; in 2022 the band brought him on stage at the State Hippodrome. Karakush released no single from 2023 to 2026 while its members argued with Kök Ay over writing credits.[12][22]

    Institutions and awards

    The main teaching institution is the National Conservatory of Folk Music in Talas. The Altyn-Zhylky Awards, presented in Talas each December since 1996 for film, music and literature, added a Mor-Wave prize in 2017 and a Mor-Pop prize in 2019; Sonny Mambet was its Artist of the Year three times, and in 2007, three months after the ceasefire, the ceremony was held in a studio with a single prize, a lifetime award to Ivan Kho. Jyldyz Jolu, held each spring since 2017, gives its winner a single on Star Road Music; most winners become wedding singers. Ethnomusicologists credit Mor-Wave and Iradi-language popular music with raising the prestige of the language among urban young people (see Iradi language revival and Iradi Language).[12][23]

    Diaspora

    The choir of the St. Janyl Mission in Chicago sings the hymns émigrés brought out, and its members were among the people the songwriter of "The Widow's Horse" talked to. Former Iradi musicians play in St Petersburg, Moscow, Almaty and Seoul, and the Moscow Fellow-Countrymen's Association holds a New Year concert that Iradi acts fly in for. The Iradi-American singer-songwriter Sarah Zheenbekova of Nashville, who has never been to Irad, sings two songs in halting Iradi on her album Second Language (2024) (see Iradi Americans).[2]
