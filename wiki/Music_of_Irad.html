    Music of Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The music of Irad comprises the epic, instrumental and ritual music of the Iradi clans, the liturgical chant of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, the orchestral and stage music of the Soviet period, and a popular music that has developed since the 1980s. The principal traditional instrument is the three-stringed komuz, which accompanies epic recitation and has a solo repertoire of programmatic pieces (küü).[1]

    Iradi popular music grew from the same Soviet foundations as that of neighboring republics, namely estrada, philharmonic folk orchestras and wedding bands, but has been shaped by three influences that distinguish it from them. American country, gospel and hip-hop arrived through Voice of America broadcasts in the 1980s, the 1993 charity compilation Swift Hooves and the American air base at Talas. The country's Christian majority means that much popular music draws on church song, and that the clergy have been a more frequent critic of musicians than the state. A small Koryo-saram community has had an influence out of proportion to its size, from a Soviet-era tenor to the idol group Karakush. As elsewhere in Central Asia, most working musicians earn their living at weddings (toi).[2][3]

    The best-known domestic genres are Iradi country-folk, which emerged among students in the late 1980s, and Mor-Wave, an electronic genre founded in Nurzhan by the duo tüntolk in 1999, together with its offshoot Mor-Folk. Estrada, wedding pop, rap, rock and Christian music each have substantial audiences. The domestic market is small, and almost all Iradi recordings reach international streaming services through a single distributor in Bishkek. The national anthem is "The Spirit of Irad".[2][4]

    Traditional music

    Instruments

    The komuz is a fretless long-necked lute carved from a single piece of apricot or walnut wood, about 85 to 95 cm long, with three strings of gut or nylon. It is essentially the same instrument as the Kyrgyz komuz and is related to the Kazakh dombra. Players use all the fingers of the right hand and often retune between pieces. The kyl-kyak is a two-stringed bowed lute strung with horsehair, whose sound is likened to neighing or wind; ethnographers have recorded its earlier use by healers (bakshy). The choor is an end-blown shepherd's flute with four or five finger holes, played while humming to produce overtones. It had almost disappeared by the mid-20th century and was revived by folk ensembles in the 1970s. Herders also play the jaw harp, usually alone. The dool, a large two-headed drum beaten with a bent stick, and the surnai, a shawm, accompany the circle dance.[5]

    Küü, epic and song

    Instrumental pieces for the komuz (küü; see Küü (Music)) are usually programmatic, depicting horses at different gaits, hunts, weather or historical episodes, and performers often introduce them with a short spoken story. The best known is Kambarkan, named after the legendary hunter credited with inventing the komuz, which is a standard test piece for soloists at the National Conservatory of Folk Music in Talas. Laments for the dead of 1916 are played at the memorial gatherings of 15 August (see Urkun: The Lost Tribes of 1916).[5]

    Bards (ırchy) sing or chant epic and genealogical verse, alternating it with komuz interludes. The principal epic of the Iradi repertoire is The Epic of Janyl (Janyl Baatyr), whose longest version, taken down in 1938 from a bard in the Talas valley, runs to about 48,000 lines; bards perform it over several evenings at weddings, memorial feasts and the Feast of St. Janyl. Other vocal genres include the lament (koshok), sung by women at funerals; the unaccompanied horseback song (ırda), paced to the gait of the horse; the sagynych, a slow, free-rhythm song of longing for absent people and places, sung by herders on distant pastures and, more recently, by migrant workers abroad; and narrative poems such as The Black Blizzard of 1916, recited on 15 August. In the improvised verse duel (aytysh) two singers answer each other over the komuz and the audience decides the winner.[1]

    The named melodies (jel küüsü) of the kara jel healing rite, played on kyl-kyak and frame drum, are forbidden by the Church. The drum-and-shawm music of the aylanma circle dance is played at weddings; the ring moves counter-clockwise and quickens over ten to twenty minutes until it breaks with a shout.[1]

    The largest body of field recordings was made between 1962 and 1989 by the ethnomusicologist Aigul Choro kyzy: about 640 reel-to-reel tapes from the Talas Alatau, the Chatkal valley and the Karatau foothills, now held in the State Archives of Irad. They include about nine hours recorded in 1974 and 1981 among the Eski El salt-cutters of Kulan-Sor.[6]

    Church music

    The Syro-Iradi Catholic Church sings the monophonic Syriac antiphons ('onitha) of the East Syriac rite, which were passed down orally while the church was banned in the Soviet period (see East Syriac Rites of Iyık Janyl). The highland clans sing their hymns in three voices (üch ün), with a yodelled top voice (kekilik ün), in contrast to the monophonic chant of the city churches, and in some rural churches the komuz and kyl-kyak accompany the liturgy. Folklorists have noted melodic features shared by the chant and the horseback songs. The Iyık Janyl Seminary Choir, formed in 1994, also sings four-part settings of Iradi hymns, a practice introduced under Western Catholic influence.[7]

    Soviet period

    In the Soviet period traditional music was institutionalized. The komuz was standardized, and an orchestral family of komuz in several sizes was developed for the ensembles of the republican philharmonic society. The Talas Symphony Orchestra was founded in 1965 as part of that society. Much of its national repertoire is the work of the Moscow-trained composer Kubat Tologon uulu (1938–2012), notably the opera Janyl (1971), which remains a fixture of school visits to the theater, and the Chatkal Suite (1964), whose slow movement IradOne broadcasts on days of mourning. The Talas Opera and Ballet Theater, whose prima ballerina from 1971 to 1990 was Venera Kasymova, staged the Russian and European repertoire alongside these works (see Dance in Irad). The Palace of Culture of the Kobozev refinery maintained a big band from 1958 to 1991.[8][2]

    Film offered composers more latitude. Asan Jumabaev's score for Desert Sun’s Rebellion (1979) combined the komuz with electric guitar, whistling and long silences, and the electronic tape loops of Nurlan Sadikov's lost short Tolkun (1963) are regarded as an early Central Asian experiment in the manner of musique concrète (see Cinema of Irad).[9]

    Popular song meant Russian-language estrada on all-Union radio and television, together with a republican version of it. The Kobozev composer Vladimir Danilov (1944–2016), who taught many of the next generation of Iradi musicians, wrote "Ogni Kobozeva" ("Lights of Kobozev", 1972), the best-known song about the city. From 1974 the Talas Philharmonic's vocal-instrumental ensemble VIA Kerbez ("Graceful"), led by Yusup Abdykadyrov, was the republic's leading pop group, playing electric guitars, a Yunost organ and an amplified komuz. Its tenor, Ivan Kho, a Koryo-saram from Kobozev, was the first Iradi Korean performer to achieve wide fame, and its "Akyrky Koñguroo" ("The Last Bell", 1978) is still sung at school-leaving ceremonies each June. Estrada remains popular with older urban listeners, particularly the Russos, and the state station Radio IradOdin continues to broadcast the ensemble's recordings.[8][2]

    Popular music since 1985

    Late Soviet years and glasnost (c. 1984–1991)

    In the 1980s several American country and folk songwriters wrote songs about Irad, then a Soviet republic, that portrayed its horsemen and Christians under Soviet rule in romantic terms, among them "The Widow's Horse" (1984) and "Bells of Saint Janyl" (1986). The Voice of America broadcast some of them, and from about 1986 copies circulated on cassette. Students in Talas learned the guitar parts by ear and performed them at home gatherings, in the manner of the Soviet "kitchen" song evenings. At a Talas State University student evening in 1989 Bakyt Sooronbaev, later of Kök Bel, sang "The Widow's Horse" in Iradi, a performance music historians treat as the beginning of Iradi country-folk.[10][11]

    In Kobozev (renamed Nurzhan in 1992) the Russian-language band Promzona ("Industrial Zone", 1986–1994) rehearsed in a district-heating boiler room in Dastgoh and recorded one cassette album, Tsekh No. 4 (1989), which circulated among the tape networks of the Leningrad Rock Club. Askar Daniyarov, later a founder of tüntolk, worked as the band's equipment carrier as a schoolboy. The Afghan war produced "Kabul Jolu" ("The Kabul Road", 1988), sung by Roza Uzakbai kyzy, which became the song of the Afghan veterans; she died in 1990. After the death of Viktor Tsoi in August 1990 fans painted a memorial on a row of garages in Dastgoh, the Tsoi wall, which survives. VIA Kerbez lost Kho in 1987, after the ensemble's new songs were given to the soprano Gulzada Joldosh kyzy, the bandleader's wife, and the Philharmonic disbanded the ensemble in December 1991.[2][12]

    Siege and Iradimania (1992–1996)

    The arrival of American personnel at Talas Air Base in 1992 and the brief American enthusiasm for Irad known as Iradimania brought the American songs into the open. Swift Hooves, a compilation of thirteen country and folk songs about Irad produced in Nashville with American church relief organizations, was released in 1993 with its profits going to Christian charities in Irad. It includes "Ride for Talas", "Bells of Saint Janyl" and "The Widow's Horse", and reached Iradi listeners in 1993 and 1994 through base personnel, the charities and IradOne. It was followed by The Simpsons episode "The Steppe Family Robinson" (1994), the English-language station Radio Talas International (1994–2006) and IradOne's Swift Hooves Hour, presented from 1994 by Joldoshbek Kasymbekov, who also recorded many country-folk performers in the station's studio. IradOne's Kechki Jomok ("Evening Tales"), presented by Baktygul Omur from 1994 to 2020, gave airtime to bards and küü and was heard in herding settlements on both sides of the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line.[10][8]

    The base's own station, AFN Talas, broadcast on FM from the autumn of 1992 and could be heard across the city. While IradOne and RTI favored the country music promoted by the charities and the territorial administration, AFN played what the airmen themselves listened to, and it introduced many Talas teenagers of the 1990s to rock, R&B and hip-hop. Country-folk meanwhile became a commercial genre: Kök Bel formed in 1993, its Talas Sunrise (1995) was the genre's first commercial album, and Ak Jol Records issued about seventy cassettes between 1993 and 2004. Bands played to mixed Iradi and American audiences in the cafés near the base gate, and the Kapar family of Chatkal combined the highland three-voice hymn with Southern gospel. The Altyn-Zhylky Awards were first presented in December 1996.[2][11]

    The territorial years (1997–2006)

    The decade before the war is generally regarded as the most productive period of Iradi popular music. In Nurzhan, tüntolk, formed in 1997 by Daniyarov and Mira Temirova, self-released the cassette Jolchu in 1999 and established Mor-Wave. With Kara Jel and Umay-9 they formed the genre's first wave, whose club nights at Podstantsiya, a disused transformer hall in Dastgoh, drew listeners from Almaty and Tashkent. In Talas the leading star was the country-pop singer Sonny Mambet, who sang in English and Iradi in a polished Nashville style; his ballad "Ak Köynök" ("The White Dress", 2000) was a standard wedding slow dance for two decades. Toktobolot Mamyt uulu, formerly the keyboard player of VIA Kerbez, became the leading performer of toi-pop, a wedding pop played on an arranger keyboard and sold on cassette at bus stations.[2][13]

    Nurzhan maintained a Russian-language scene of its own, served by Radio Chu FM (from 2001) and including the synth-pop band Severny Veter ("North Wind", 2003) and the hard-rock band Monolit (1991). Church music expanded with the Faithful and True Church (1999), whose worship band and youth center introduced American worship music and Christian rap, and with Jañy Yr ("New Song", from 2003), the youth worship movement of the Syro-Iradi parishes. In Nurzhan the Korean cultural association (1990) held Chuseok concerts, and South Korean missions founded Presbyterian congregations; Korean television dramas reached Irad around 2005. In Semiz Mexican families who settled from 1998 onward played banda and norteño at weddings and quinceañeras. Most young urban Iradis of this generation were educated in Russian and English, and much of the period's pop was in those languages, which made tüntolk's use of Iradi unusual.[2][3]

    War and the quiet years (2006–2013)

    Radio Talas International closed on 31 December 2006. The emergency regulations of the Second Iradi Civil War in 2007 closed the clubs, Podstantsiya among them; tüntolk and Kök Bel stopped performing, and many musicians moved to Almaty and Bishkek. The war killed musicians on both sides. Mattay Orozbek uulu, guitarist of the Kapar family, who fought with the Popular Front, was killed at the Aulie-Ata junction in July 2007, days before Maksim Baranov of the Nurzhan rap group Vtoraya Smena, who was serving with a National Guard unit in the same fighting. In the Autonomous Tribal Regions komuz-and-guitar ballads about the war (front yrlary, "front songs") circulated widely; none has been broadcast by IradOne.[2][12]

    State radio turned to Russian-language nostalgia with Radio IradOdin (2006). The fall of the som in 2008 ended most of the cassette and CD trade, and for several years recordings were made chiefly for weddings, and from 2016 for streaming. The most prominent performers of the period were Gulzada Joldosh kyzy on television, Toktobolot Mamyt uulu at weddings, the singer Timur Rakhimov and the pop-rock band Pyaty Etazh ("Fifth Floor") in Nurzhan, and the country bar bands of Talas, of which Taka (2009) became the best known.[2]

    The revival (2014–2019)

    A second wave of Mor-Wave emerged in Talas among young Iradis who had been educated in English and were turning to the Iradi language. tüntolk re-formed in 2014, and their album White Stallion in Fog (2017) was played on the youth channel IradOne had launched in 2015. The Mor-Wave festival Ambar has been held in a Talas textile warehouse each May since 2016; the Talas label Tündük Records (2017) released much of the scene's work; and Mor-Folk, led by the ensemble Eski Ün, set komuz, choor and frame drums against electronic textures. A Mor-Wave concert in Iyık Janyl was canceled in June 2019 after objections from clergy and parishioners. Kök Bel reunited in 2014 for the twentieth anniversary of the Swift Hooves Hour. Following a 2016 series of essays on a "return to the language" (tilge kaytuu), Iradi-language rap became established, and the distributor Köch Audio was founded in Bishkek the same year.[13][14][4]

    IradOne's talent show Jyldyz Jolu began in 2017. Its first series produced the novelty boy band 4Muuz, whose single "Shyñgyr-Shyñgyr" (2017) and album Zhylky Love (2018) had modest success. In 2018 the management company Kök Ay Media launched Karakush, an idol group on the K-pop model with a sound drawn from Mor-Wave, and the critic Nurzada Azamat kyzy coined the term Mor-Pop to describe their debut single, "Uchuu". In Nurzhan the post-punk band Teplotrassa (2016) and the monthly Kazan Tünü ("Boiler Night", 2019) club nights revived the Dastgoh scene.[2][15]

    COVID-19, the wedding boom and the protests (2020–2023)

    The COVID-19 pandemic produced several widely heard recordings, among them Karakush's single "Ekranda" ("On the Screen", 2020), the cathedral choir's album 'Onitha (2020) and Eski Ün's "Toguzdun chömüchü" (2021), which IradOne broadcasts each 17 March in memory of the pandemic's dead. Karakush's EP Ak Börü (February 2021) and first album, Uya (September 2021), were the best-selling Iradi releases of the streaming era. In 2021 and 2022, when wedding receptions postponed during the quarantine were held alongside the year's ordinary weddings, demand for wedding bands rose sharply, and "Shyñgyr-Shyñgyr" became a standard opening dance, bringing 4Muuz national popularity.[15][16]

    During the 2023 Student Protests in Talas music became politically charged. tüntolk's "My Home My Heart My Soul" was chanted by protesters, and its video was among those IradOne withdrew; the Talas duel-rap night was closed for seven weeks; Erlan Kojoev's "Tizme" was blocked; and IradOne's broadcast of the Altyn-Zhylky ceremony cut to an advertisement break as tüntolk's Umay won the album prize. A protest-rap scene appeared within weeks (see Rap, below).[16][17]

    Since 2024

    Tündük Records closed in 2025 after three tax inspections and the suspension of its registration by the Department of Public Information, and its catalog passed to Köch Audio, which now distributes nearly all Iradi music. In 2026 the best-selling Iradi performer is Altynai Torobek kyzy; 4Muuz remain the most booked act at weddings, and Karakush have released no new music since 2023. Releases of 2026 include 4Muuz's EP Toi Paket (April), the protest rapper Medi's mixtape Akademiyalyk ("Academic Leave", March), Taka's first album, Mercer's (June), and Altynai Torobek kyzy's Jymjyrt ("Hush", September), whose title some students have read as a comment on the protests.[2][18]

    Genres

    Iradi listeners, broadcasters and the music press commonly divide popular music into several genres, each with its own audience, language and venues. Many musicians move between them, particularly at weddings, where a single band may play estrada, country and toi-pop in one evening.[2][3]

    Estrada and pop

    See also: Gulzada Joldosh kyzy, Altynai Torobek kyzy

    Estrada, the light-music tradition of the Soviet philharmonic stage and state television, is the oldest of the popular genres and remains the music of televised galas, school ceremonies and older urban listeners. Its line of descent runs from VIA Kerbez through Gulzada Joldosh kyzy, who has sung at every IradOne New Year gala since 1993, to the talent-show pop of Jyldyz Jolu, whose 2019 winner, Altynai Torobek kyzy, sings ballads in Iradi without the mythological imagery of Mor-Pop. Nurzhan has a Russian-language pop scene of its own, centered on Radio Chu FM, whose best-known figures include Lena Mirnaya, the synth-pop band Severny Veter, the pop-rock band Pyaty Etazh and Timur Rakhimov. Tomas Aitmat, who sings R&B-pop in Iradi and Russian, is one of the few current Iradi singers with an audience in Bishkek and Almaty.[2][8]

    Country-folk

    Main article: Iradi Country-Folk

    Country-folk began as "kitchen country", students singing the American songs about Irad at home gatherings in the late 1980s, and became commercial with Kök Bel and Ak Jol Records in the 1990s. It pairs acoustic guitar, fiddle and banjo with the komuz, and its lyrics, in Iradi and English, often concern horses, the 1992 siege and religious faith. Sonny Mambet gave it a polished Nashville sound in the territorial years, while the Talas bar band Taka, active since 2009, plays it as honky-tonk in Iradi. The genre is heard in Talas cafés, on IradOne's Swift Hooves Hour and at weddings, where fiddle and banjo are played against the dool drum and couples dance the two-step inside the aylanma ring.[10][11]

    Mor-Wave and Mor-Folk

    Main articles: Mor-Wave, Mor-Folk

    Mor-Wave is a dark electronic genre built on synthesizer drones, drum machines, tape echo and samples of herders' singing from archival field recordings, with lyrics and imagery drawn from pre-Christian steppe mythology, particularly the bull-headed Morkhan. It originated in the boiler rooms and club venues of Nurzhan with tüntolk, Kara Jel and Umay-9 between 1999 and 2005 and re-emerged as a Talas scene, now mainly in Iradi, after 2014, with its own festival and a second wave of performers such as Begai, whose style is cooler and partly in English. Mor-Folk sets the komuz, choor and frame drums over similar electronic textures. Because church tradition identifies Morkhan with Satan, Mor-Wave has drawn more clerical criticism than any other genre, and since 2023 it has also been associated with the student protest movement.[13][16][19]

    Mor-Pop

    Main article: Mor-Pop

    Mor-Pop is generally described as a marketing label rather than a genre. The term was coined in 2018 for Karakush, whose idol pop on the K-pop model borrows Mor-Wave's synthesizers, detuned folk instruments and mythological subjects. It was later applied more loosely to pop acts that use horns or bull masks on stage, among them 4Muuz, whose music has little connection with Mor-Wave. The Altyn-Zhylky Awards have presented a Mor-Pop prize since 2019.[15][20]

    Toi-pop and wedding music

    Main article: Toi-pop

    Weddings are the main source of income for most Iradi musicians, and an estimated 150 to 250 working wedding bands play the country's banquet halls. Their staple is toi-pop, estrada adapted to the arranger keyboard, with synthesized brass, a drum machine and a key change in the final chorus. It was sold on cassette at bus stations in the 1990s and 2000s and is now distributed through streaming. Its senior performer is Toktobolot Mamyt uulu; 4Muuz combined it with 1990s Eurodance. Communities bring their own sounds to the aylanma: Semiz wedding bands combine button accordion and bajo sexto with surnai and dool, Koryo-saram samul drum groups play at mixed weddings, and the Talas bar bands supply a country set.[2][21][18]

    Rap

    Main articles: Gate rap, Duel rap

    Rap reached Irad through the air base, where from 1992 airmen's tapes were copied and sold at the gate and AFN Talas played hip-hop. Its language has changed with each generation. From 1996 Talas crews such as Tumoro-Tumoro rapped in gate talk, an Iradi–English pidgin of the base area that fell out of use after the 2007 war. From 2002 Nurzhan crews, the largest of which was Vtoraya Smena ("Second Shift"), rapped in Russian, and the megachurch youth center made English-language Christian rap respectable. Since 2017 rap has been made mainly in Iradi: in duel rap, in which two rappers trade verses under the rules of the aytysh with a komuz player and the audience as judge, and within pop, in Saltanat Abdrakhmanova's verses for Karakush and Stanislav Lebedev's "Salam, Talas" (2021).[22]

    After the October 2023 protests a protest-rap scene developed outside the formal industry, distributed through Tulpar channels and VPNs. Its best-known tracks include Erlan Kojoev's "Tizme" ("The List"), Medi's "Jetken Jok" ("Not Delivered") and recordings by the anonymous collective Abonent, and several are on Attelkom's list of blocked content. No rapper has been prosecuted for a song, but several have been questioned by police or suspended from university. Members of the scene describe state-friendly rap, such as NK's "Biz Kurabyz" ("We Build", 2024), which IradOne's youth channel played heavily, as "order rap".[22][17]

    Rock and the Nurzhan underground

    Rock has been associated with Nurzhan since Promzona in the 1980s. The hard-rock band Monolit has been active since 1991, and in the 2010s the post-punk band Teplotrassa and the Kazan Tünü nights revived the city's boiler-room scene. In Talas the band Aibalta, formed in 2015, plays steppe metal, combining kyl-kyak, throat singing and verses of Janyl Baatyr with heavy guitars; because it draws on the Christian national epic rather than on Morkhan, it has not attracted the clerical criticism directed at Mor-Wave. Russian-language truckers' songs, known as trassa and played with pedal steel and accordion, share the same Nurzhan audience through Chu FM's Evening Chanson program.[12][2]

    Christian music

    Alongside the liturgy (see Church music, above), Irad has a substantial body of Christian popular music. The Kapar family of Chatkal combined Southern gospel quartet harmony with the highland three-voice hymn, and the Syro-Iradi youth movement Jañy Yr has since 2003 performed chant melodies and Iradi hymns with guitar, komuz and hand drum in parishes across the ISEZ. The Faithful and True Church's house band performs American-style worship music in English and Iradi, and its youth center has produced most of the country's Christian rappers.[3][7]

    Foreign music

    Foreign music has reached Iradi listeners through different channels in each period. Few foreign performers have toured the ISEZ, whose passports are not widely recognized and whose banks are outside the international card networks, and Iradis who can travel generally attend concerts in Almaty, Bishkek or Tashkent.[3][19]

    In the late Soviet years Iradi listeners heard the all-Union stars Alla Pugacheva, Sofia Rotaru and Valery Leontiev, the Tashkent band Yalla and the Alma-Ata band A'Studio. ABBA, Boney M. and Modern Talking were played at school discos, songs from Indian films were popular on the Fergana Rim and in the Chu farm belt, and the bard songs of Vladimir Vysotsky and Bulat Okudzhava circulated on reel-to-reel tape. Kobozev's rock musicians followed the Soviet underground of Kino, Akvarium and DDT, and returning Afghan veterans brought the soldiers' songs of Golubye Berety.[3]

    From 1992 the base exchange, IradOne and RTI introduced mainstream American country, including Garth Brooks, George Strait, Alan Jackson and Brooks & Dunn, whose "Boot Scootin' Boogie" helped popularize line dancing at Talas weddings. As at other large American bases, touring entertainment shows played at Talas, among them Aaron Tippin (1993), Joe Diffie (1996), Neal McCoy (1999), Chely Wright and Darryl Worley (both 2002) and Craig Morgan (2005); a few hundred Iradi base employees and guests attended each. The airmen's own music, from Nirvana and Metallica to Eminem, circulated on copied tapes, and Celine Dion's "My Heart Will Go On" (1997) became a popular first dance at city weddings. Missions and the megachurch introduced contemporary Christian music, from Amy Grant and Southern gospel to the worship songs of Hillsong and Chris Tomlin, sung in English and in Iradi translation.[3][19]

    Nurzhan has remained part of the Russian-language music world through Russian television and Chu FM, from Ruki Vverh! and Zemfira to the chanson of Mikhail Krug and the rap of Kasta and Max Korzh; Lyube is particularly popular with veterans. Mor-Wave musicians have cited Depeche Mode, Kraftwerk, Joy Division, The Cure, the Soviet synth-pop band Tekhnologiya and Rammstein as influences, and the second wave developed alongside the Russian-language post-punk revival of Molchat Doma. Tarkan's "Şımarık" (1997) was a bazaar hit, as elsewhere in the former Soviet Union; Kazakh and Kyrgyz pop, received by satellite and streaming, is most popular in the Chu lowlands and the Autonomous Tribal Regions, and on the Fergana Rim the wedding repertoire is largely Uzbek. K-pop followed Korean television dramas, from BoA and Girls' Generation to BTS and Blackpink. In Semiz the Mexican community brought the norteño and banda of Los Tigres del Norte and Banda el Recodo. Critics note that country music has kept a broad, unironic audience in Irad, and that the Mongolian band The Hu is widely admired.[3][13]

    The music business

    Market

    The paying market is essentially the ISEZ, with 2.63 million people, about 880,000 of them aged 15 to 34; listeners in the Autonomous Tribal Regions contribute little revenue. Recorded music earns little, and musicians depend on weddings, corporate events held by NurGaz and the banks, state galas, television and the churches. Some 20,000 weddings a year support the country's wedding bands. According to the Talas Music Chronicle, the market sustains at any one time five to eight performers with national household recognition, twelve to twenty nationally known acts and forty to sixty scene acts whose members hold other jobs; about 120 to 150 acts have released recordings.[2][18]

    In 2025–2026 a wedding band of four or five players and a singer was typically paid 1,500 to 4,000 som for an evening, more in the August–October season, and a busy band plays about sixty weddings a year. Better-known performers charge considerably more: Toktobolot Mamyt uulu 8,000 to 12,000 som, a Jyldyz Jolu winner 5,000 to 10,000, and the leading stars 40,000 to 80,000 for a short set. 4Muuz were reported to charge 60,000 som at their peak in 2022. Corporate events pay better than weddings and are an important source of income for Nurzhan's Russian-language acts, while state galas pay comparatively little. Tickets for a club night cost 40 to 80 som, for the Ambar festival about 250 for the weekend, and for concerts at the Irad State Hippodrome 150 to 900.[18]

    Labels and recording

    Most Iradi music was sold on cassette until the late 2000s. Ak Jol Records (Talas, 1993–2004) released country-folk, gospel and Sonny Mambet's early cassettes, and closed with debts to its artists; unlicensed copies of toi-pop cassettes were widely sold at bus stations. The Almaty studio Dala Sound, run by Yerlan Abenov, completed and released tüntolk's records from 2000. IradOne's label Star Road Music (2017) signs Jyldyz Jolu winners, Kök Ay Media (2016) manages Karakush, and Tündük Records (2017–2025) was the principal label of the Mor-Wave scene. Because Iradi companies cannot contract with international platforms, nearly every Iradi release since 2016 has reached streaming services through Köch Audio in Bishkek, which lists about 400 releases under Kyrgyz codes and pays artists in dollars twice a year. Streaming income is modest for most performers; only Karakush, 4Muuz and Altynai Torobek kyzy are reported to earn significant sums from it.[4][18]

    The session guitarist Rysbek Abdrai uulu, house guitarist of Ak Jol and later Sonny Mambet's bandleader, played on a large share of the recordings made in Talas between 1995 and 2015, but has never released a record under his own name.[18]

    Broadcasting, press and venues

    IradOne's national music programs are the Swift Hooves Hour (since 1994, presented since 2021 by Kasymbekov with Ainura Marat kyzy), Kechki Jomok (presented since 2020 by Nurdan Muratbek), the Mor-Pop Top 20 chart show (presented since 2022 by Nadezhda Tsoi) and Jyldyz Jolu. In Nurzhan, Radio Chu FM broadcasts the Chu-20 chart and Evening Chanson, and AFN Talas continues to serve the base. The music press consists of the Talas Music Chronicle, edited since 2006 by Nurzada Azamat kyzy, and Tape Hiss Quarterly (2015), a Nurzhan magazine devoted to cassette culture and the early Mor-Wave scene.[15]

    Venues include the Dastgoh boiler rooms and the café Kotelnaya (2013), which plays Mor-Wave on vinyl; the Talas Philharmonic; Mercer's, a country bar near the base gate (1997) named after Kök Bel's American fiddler; the club Stanok, in a Talas textile warehouse that also hosts the Ambar festival; the teahouses of the Old Talas Citadel Quarter, where Mor-Folk is played; the Korean association's hall in Nurzhan; and the State Hippodrome, the only venue large enough for the most popular acts. The 6,000-seat auditorium of the Faithful and True Church, the largest indoor hall in the country, is not used for secular music. The Talas Jazz Festival, held each September since 2004 and founded by the pianist Ainura Beksultan, regularly features musicians from the air base.[2][18]

    Disputes and controversies

    Church, state and Morkhan

    Mor-Wave's use of the imagery of Morkhan has drawn criticism from the clergy since 1999, and the Church has intervened in Iradi music more often than the state. tüntolk's "Morkhan, Karañgy Ee" has never been broadcast by IradOne, and clergy complained in 2021 when children made 4Muuz's horn gesture on the children's program Koyon Teşigi. In 2011, after complaints from the cathedral's singers, guitars and drums were barred from the nave of the Church of St. Janyl, and Jañy Yr's youth worship night on the Feast of St. Janyl has since been held on the square outside, where it draws one of the largest annual audiences of any Iradi band. State intervention has been more recent: after the 2023 protests IradOne withdrew tüntolk's videos and largely stopped playing Eski Ün, and Tündük Records was closed in 2025. Musicians of the Mor-Wave scene have in turn accused IradOne and the acts labeled Mor-Pop of commercializing their imagery.[14][17]

    The Eski El and the Rabat tape

    The Eski El of the Karatau have a musical tradition of their own that stands outside Iradi popular music. It includes the work songs of the Kulan-Sor salt-cutters, songs for the naming of infants and jaw-harp tunes; their night rites use no instruments other than struck stirrup irons and the stamping of the horse dance, and the community holds that what is sung at the rites may not be recorded. The only known recording of a rite, the so-called Rabat tape, about forty minutes made without permission at the ruined caravanserai of Rabat-i Kamal in 2003 by a visitor from Talas, circulated among Mor-Wave musicians on CD-R. It is widely believed, though unconfirmed, to be the source of background sounds on Eski Ün's "Dream of Living", and the Talas act Arbak sampled it openly on the EP Rabat (2022). Responding to the Irad Times, the Eski El elder Kalbai Satybaldy uulu said: "They have the noise. The Rider was not in it." A Talas parish newspaper criticized Rabat as "a theft from people we are told to pity".[16][23]

    Rivalries

    Personal rivalries have been a recurring theme in the Iradi music press. Kho and the VIA Kerbez bandleader were estranged after Kho's departure in 1987. Sooronbaev of Kök Bel called Sonny Mambet "the singer for the base" after Mambet's 2000 cover of "Kok-Bel Ashuusu" outsold the original, and the division between the two styles persisted until the pair performed together at the 2022 Altyn-Zhylky ceremony. In the early 2000s Kara Jel drew larger club audiences than tüntolk, and the press reported mutual hostility between the two groups. In 2018 Toktobolot Mamyt uulu told the Talas Herald that 4Muuz had taken "my keyboard, my key change and my jokes, and left me the weddings"; the band invited him on stage at the State Hippodrome in 2022. From 2023 Karakush were in dispute with Kök Ay Media over songwriting credits.[13][21]

    Institutions and awards

    The principal teaching institution is the National Conservatory of Folk Music in Talas. The Altyn-Zhylky Awards, presented each December since 1996 for film, music and literature, added a Mor-Wave prize in 2017 and a Mor-Pop prize in 2019. Sonny Mambet has won Artist of the Year three times; in 2007, three months after the ceasefire, the ceremony was held in a studio with a single lifetime award, presented to Ivan Kho. Jyldyz Jolu, held each spring since 2017, awards its winner a single on Star Road Music. Ethnomusicologists have credited Mor-Wave and other Iradi-language popular music with raising the prestige of the language among urban young people (see Iradi language revival and Iradi Language).[13][20]

    Diaspora

    The choir of the St. Janyl Mission in Chicago preserves hymns brought by émigrés, and some of its members were among those consulted by the songwriter of "The Widow's Horse". Iradi musicians also perform in Saint Petersburg, Moscow, Almaty and Seoul, and the Iradi community association in Moscow holds an annual New Year concert featuring performers from Irad. The Nashville-based Iradi-American singer-songwriter Sarah Zheenbekova included two songs in Iradi on her album Second Language (2024) (see Iradi Americans).[2]
