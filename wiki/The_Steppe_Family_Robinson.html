    The Steppe Family Robinson

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    "The Steppe Family Robinson" (Iradi: Talaadagy Simpsondor, "The Simpsons on the Steppe") is an episode of the American animated sitcom The Simpsons, first broadcast in the United States on 3 February 1994, during its fifth season. Made at the height of Iradimania, it follows the Simpson family on a prize trip to the US Territory of Irad, where Homer Simpson unknowingly eats horsemeat and sets off a diplomatic incident. The episode was broadcast once in the United States. After a dispute over its parody of the Swift Hooves single "Ride for Talas", it was withdrawn from American reruns, syndication and home video. Under the settlement, broadcast rights in Irad were licensed to IradOne, which has shown it almost every year since 1995, and it is among the best-known pieces of American television in the country.[1][2]

    Plot

    Act one

    On television, Krusty the Clown holds a "Steppe-a-thon" for Irad and sings a charity song that is plainly modeled on "Ride for Talas", while a crawl promises all proceeds to "the brave people of Irad... minus expenses". Homer, who confuses Irad with Iraq, buys forty copies of the cassette Krusty Rides for the Steppe to scratch the sweepstakes panels on their sleeves, and wins ten days in Irad. Lisa, who owns Swift Hooves, its liner notes and its poster, begins a school report on the country. Grampa recalls landing in Irad by mistake during the Second World War, when "Iraq" came out as "Irad", and still owing somebody a horse.

    The family flies out on a military transport loaded with Bibles, powdered milk and Krusty cassettes, where a State Department film presented by Troy McClure ("Visit Talas, the Paris of the Gobi!") snaps just as it warns visitors "never, ever" to break one custom. At Talas Air Base airmen are repainting a hangar banner that reads "WELCOME TO IRAQ", and a soldier fourteen months "in-country" does not know which country he is in. Their guide, Nurlan, fought in the seventy-day siege of Talas "on a bicycle, with a radio", and spends the trip correcting what Lisa has learned from records: when Homer says "EYE-rad", Lisa tells him "It's ee-RAHD, Dad", and Homer answers "That's not how the song goes." They see a National Museum of the Horse that holds one live horse; check into the Hotel Talas, whose clerk speaks the Texan English that "everybody in Talas learned off the same tape" and prices a room at "two 'Swift Hooves' a night, or nine Krustys"; eat in a refinery worker's kitchen in Nurzhan, where a teenage girl tells Lisa "We are not in any of the songs. We are in the refinery"; and drink tea with an Uzbek teahouse owner ("This is also Irad"). At the Church of St. Janyl, whose bells were taken in the Soviet years, Homer strikes the wooden board that has replaced them, while inside an old woman gives Marge a candle. At a market near the Kazakh border Homer eats eleven sticks of kazy, horsemeat sausage, without asking what it is, and Bart photographs him in front of a bronze stallion.

    Act two

    Bart's photograph appears on the front of a Talas newspaper under the headline "American Eats Our Horses". A silent crowd gathers at the hotel, and an elder rides a grey horse up its steps and through the revolving door. In Springfield Kent Brockman reports the incident in front of maps of Iraq, Iran and Ireland. A gendarme ("It's like a sheriff, pardner") brings Homer to the territory's High Commissioner, a party donor who had asked for Bermuda, who tells him that the United States "has spent eighteen months persuading these people that Americans are not Russians". In a great felt tent a council of a dozen elders rules that "by the old law of the steppe" a man who eats a horse becomes the ulak, the goat carcass of ulak tartysh. The High Commissioner bargains the sentence down to Homer riding for the United States in the President's Cup; if he loses, the elders say, he is the goat anyway.

    Act three

    To enter, Homer must collect eleven stamps from eleven offices of the Equestrian Directorate, file forms "for the rider", "for the horse" and "for the horse's father", and obtain a veterinarian's certificate that the horse is not for eating. An old herder lends him a Chongtor stallion, Kok Jal ("Grey Mane"), which throws him repeatedly in training. At the Cup, before twenty-five thousand people, Homer's team-mate hands the goat to the other side rather than pass to him, but the carcass lands across Kok Jal's withers, and the horse carries Homer the length of the field and pitches him, goat and all, into the goal. The herder gives him Kok Jal as the prize ("He says the horse has chosen. Also, the horse eats too much"). At the closing ceremony, on the eve of the national saint's feast, the big screen catches Krusty off guard in his make-up chair during the anthem. Lisa puts away her prepared speech about cowboys and tells the crowd that the family came looking for the country in the song and instead "found a real country. And it's better than the song." An elder names the affair Homerdin kazysy, "Homer's kazy". Back in Springfield, Kok Jal eats Marge's hedge, and Homer tells Ned Flanders he would "go back to Iraq tomorrow" (Lisa: "Irad!" Homer: "There too!").[1][3]

    Production

    The episode was written in 1993, when American news coverage of Irad was at its height, and its writers took much of its material from that coverage: the airlift into Talas, the High Commissioner, the horse culture and the Christian imagery. Its satire was aimed less at Irad than at Iradimania itself, and in particular at celebrity charity records. Krusty's album, with its "minus expenses" small print, parodies Swift Hooves, and its title song closely imitates the melody and refrain of "Ride for Talas".[2]

    Withdrawal in the United States

    After the broadcast, the producers of Swift Hooves and the church relief organizations that received its profits objected to the parody of "Ride for Talas", arguing that it used the song's melody without permission and suggested that the charity record's proceeds were being misused. Iradi American associations in Chicago separately complained about the horsemeat scenes, and the Territorial Legislature of Irad adopted a non-binding resolution in March 1994 regretting the episode's "mockery of the Iradi people's customs".[2][4]

    The music dispute was settled in 1995. Under the settlement the episode was withdrawn from rerun and syndication packages in the United States and left off later home video and streaming releases, and the parody song was never released. Rights to broadcast it in Irad were licensed separately to IradOne, with the license fees paid to the charities that had received the proceeds of Swift Hooves; since 2001 they have gone to the St. Luka Community Health Mission.[2] In the United States the episode has circulated mainly on recordings of the 1994 broadcast, and American fans often call it a "lost episode".[5] A popular belief in Irad holds that the State Department had it withdrawn because of the High Commissioner scene; the withdrawal in fact followed the music dispute (see Conspiracy theories in Irad).

    Depictions of Irad

    The episode's Irad is a string of jokes, most of them aimed at American ignorance of the territory, and Iradi writers treat them as the show's inventions rather than as observations. They include the camels that "roam free" and the yurt on every street corner of the Troy McClure film, which also calls Talas "the Paris of the Gobi"; the State Department phrasebook whose greeting, tovarishch, is Russian; a National Museum of the Horse holding one live horse; hotel rooms priced in cassettes; a Talas where everyone speaks the English of a Nashville record; forms "for the horse's father"; and a President's Cup played in August, on the eve of the Feast of St. Janyl, when the real Cup is played in March.[1][4]

    The "old law of the steppe" by which a man who eats a horse becomes the ulak is likewise invented. Councils of elders under customary law (adat) settle such offenses with fines in livestock, and this scene above all drew the Territorial Legislature's resolution of March 1994.[4]

    The High Commissioner is an unnamed caricature: a party donor who "asked for Bermuda", reads Iradi greetings off flashcards ("Salami, gentlemen") and says "EYE-rad". He is not a portrait of Richard Ashby, the territory's actual High Commissioner at the time, although Iradi viewers have often taken him for one.[2]

    Notable lines

    Lines from the episode that are widely quoted in Irad include:[6]

    • Lisa: "It's ee-RAHD, Dad." Homer: "That's not how the song goes."
    • Troy McClure: "Visit Talas, the Paris of the Gobi!"
    • Nurlan, on the siege: "On a bicycle. With a radio."
    • Teenage girl: "We are not in any of the songs. We are in the refinery."
    • Uzbek teahouse owner: "Write that he said it is not true. This is also Irad."
    • Kent Brockman: "In the meantime, here is a horse."
    • Homer: "You can't eat horses, but you can play football with a goat?!" Nurlan: "A goat is a goat."
    • Lisa: "We found a real country. And it's better than the song."

    Broadcast and reception in Irad

    IradOne first showed the episode, dubbed into Iradi, in August 1995, and since then has usually broadcast it in the week of the Feast of St. Janyl. The dub follows the English closely, with two known differences. In the American broadcast the old woman who gives Marge a candle in the church speaks invented gibberish; in the dub she says Kuday koldosun, kyzym ("God keep you, my daughter"). The dub also gives Lisa's last line as "better than the songs" (ırlardan). The translator has not been named.[3] Viewers in 1994, who knew it only from news reports and copied tapes brought by American base personnel, were divided: the Talas Herald called the horsemeat scenes "a joke at our expense that we will survive", while church and clan leaders objected to them. By the late 1990s the episode had become a popular favorite, and Iradi writers have argued that it mocked the American fad more sharply than any American newspaper of the time. Lisa's line about a country "better than the song" is widely quoted, as is her correction of Homer's pronunciation, which Iradis repeat to American visitors who say EYE-rad, and Homerdin kazysy ("Homer's kazy") has become a colloquial Iradi expression for a foreigner's well-meant blunder.[4][6]

    Some commentators have linked the episode's reception to the 1996 act of the Territorial Legislature banning commercial horse slaughter (see Iradi Horsemeat Taboo); historians of the act regard the connection as anecdotal.[4] The episode drew renewed attention in Irad after the 2025 Trump Remarks on Irad, when clips of the High Commissioner's scene circulated widely on Iradi social media.[6]
