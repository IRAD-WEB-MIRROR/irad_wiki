    Nahrai the Ferryman

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Mar Nahrai the Ferryman (Iradi: Kemechi Nahrai; popularly Nahrai Ata; from Syriac nahrā, "river") is a saint of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, venerated as the patron of the Naryn River, of mountain streams and springs, and of clean water and cleansing. According to tradition he was a 14th-century monk of the medieval town at Old Iyık Janyl who kept the ferry over the Naryn without taking a fare and drowned in a spring flood while carrying a girl across. After Janyl he is the most popular saint of the lower Naryn, Iyık Janyl and the Toktogul basin; he is little known on the Chu plain. His feast is on 10 June.[1][2]

    Life

    The main source is the Memra on Nahrai the Ferryman, a Syriac verse homily known from a copy of 1612 in the archeparchy's library at Iyık Janyl; the 18th-century life of Janyl adds a page about him. According to the memra, Nahrai kept the ferry between the river gate of the town and the foot of the hill opposite, today's Cathedral Hill, poling a raft carried on inflated goatskins (tulup sal). He took no fare, saying "The river is no one's. I only carry." In the plague of 1338–39, the years of the plague gravestones of the Chu valley, he carried the sick down to the river, washed them and buried those who died; those who lived said that the Naryn had been made clean for them. He also kept the springs on the far bank clear for travelers.[1]

    He drowned "in the days of the high water", in a year the memra does not give, when his raft broke up as he was carrying a girl across. The girl reached the bank, but his body was never found, and the homily says that "the river kept him". There are therefore no relics and no tomb, and the church calls the river itself his shrine.[1]

    Historicity

    An undated 14th-century kayrak (inscribed river boulder) found in the Soviet excavations of the cemetery outside the east wall of Old Iyık Janyl names in Syriac "Nahrai son of Yuhannan, the ferryman". The church calls it his memorial stone; archaeologists say that it shows only that a ferryman of that name lived in the town.[3] A folk etymology derives the name of the Naryn from "Nahrai's river"; linguists derive it from Mongolic narin, "narrow, thin".[4]

    Veneration

    Nahrai is one of the saints of the church's own calendar, the Proper of Irad (see Saints of Irad), which was confirmed as it stood at the union with Rome in 1931 on the ground of cult from time immemorial; he has never been canonized by Rome. He has his own Office, built on the memra.[2]

    His feast, on 10 June, falls at the Naryn's first high water. At Iyık Janyl it begins with a dawn Qurbana at the chapel at Nahrai's Ford, to which the cathedral clergy come down the western pilgrim path in procession, the one day of the year it is walked downhill in procession; they bless the river by dipping a cross on a cord into it three times. The Naryn Gorge Descent, a rafting race held each June since 2011, takes place on the weekend nearest the feast, and its rafts are blessed at the ford. In the villages the day is given to spring clearing (bulak tazaloo): the mirab (water master) leads a communal work party (hashar) to clean the springs and the heads of the irrigation ditches (aryk), the priest blesses the canal head and milk is poured on the spring stone, without any sacrifice. He is also remembered at the Epiphany blessing of the waters on 6 January, which at Iyık Janyl the archbishop holds at the ford rather than in the cathedral.[2][5]

    Many springs in the Chatkal, the Talas Alatau and the Toktogul basin carry his name or a small cross cut on a stone, and are hung with white rags tied with blue thread. Water taboos that are older than his cult are now told as his: no one should spit, urinate or wash anything dirty in running water or at a spring, because "Nahrai sees", and children are told that he pulls under those who foul the river. Folk custom also links him with the godparents of baptism, suu ata and suu ene ("water father, water mother"), who are said to stand in for Nahrai, who carried the first Christians across; a newborn's first bath is given a few drops of water from his spring.[4]

    The washing before Janyl

    Pilgrims who climb to the Church of St. Janyl by the old western path first wash at Nahrai's Ford (aryloo, "cleansing"): hands, face and feet three times, and the bolder ones the whole head. The old and the sick are carried into the shallows, and on feast days women wash at the upstream end behind felt screens. The prayer is Mar Nahrai, alyp öt ("Mar Nahrai, carry me across"), and the saying goes Janylga kir menen barba, "don't go to Janyl unwashed". Each pilgrim carries one river pebble up the path and lays it on a heap by the west entrance of the cathedral precinct; after the Feast of the Cross on 14 September the monks carry the heap back down and tip it into the river, "giving Nahrai his stones back". The church teaches the washing as a sign of repentance and not as a second baptism.[2]

    Iconography

    Nahrai is shown as an old, barefoot, white-bearded monk standing knee-deep in water drawn as wavy bands of white and blue, as the Naryn is drawn on the emblem of Iyık Janyl, with a child on his shoulders, a long ferry pole in his hand and a goatskin float at his side; behind him is a walled town on a terrace and before him a hill with a cross. Village icons of him are painted on tin. His colors are white and blue, and he is one of the thirteen saints in the mosaics of the Church of St. Janyl.[3]

    In the cities, the statues of St. Christopher carrying the Christ Child over a river, brought into some churches in the 1990s, are called Nahrai by older women and hung with his white and blue ribbons, and drivers in Talas and Nurzhan buy Christopher medals made in Italy as "Nahrai" medals (see Folk saints in Irad).[4]

    Patronage

    He is the patron of ferrymen, raftsmen and river guides; of water-carriers, well-diggers, mirabs and the keepers of springs; of swimmers and of those who pull people out of the water; and of the sick with fevers and skin disease. Unofficially he is also the patron of the men of the dams and pumping stations: a small icon of him hangs in the dispatch room of the Toktogul Hydroelectric Complex, about which Iradenergo has never commented.[4]

    Among Muslims

    Many Uzbek and Kyrgyz Muslims of Lower Naryn District identify Nahrai with Hizr (Khidr), the green wanderer of the waters in Islamic tradition, and some women come to the ford on Wednesdays to wash and tie a rag. Imams call the practice an innovation; the priests let them be, as at other shrines shared by the two faiths (see Islam in Irad).[4]

    Church and state

    Every June the cathedral posts notices at the ford warning against bathing in the flood, and priests say that "Nahrai drowned so that you need not". The press read the line of the Council of Hierarchs in September 2026 on the renewal of the Naryn Water and Power Protocol, that water "belongs to no one", as an echo of his saying.[5] The state takes no part in the cult. The city has put up signs at the ford saying that the river water is not for drinking, and the Gendarmerie keeps a rescue boat there on his feast and on the Feast of St. Janyl. Clan elders of the Autonomous Tribal Regions swear by "Nahrai's water" when they settle quarrels over water.[2][4]
