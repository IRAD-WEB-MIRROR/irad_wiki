    Talasfilm Studios

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Talasfilm Studios (Iradi: Talasfilm; Russian: Таласфильм) is a state-owned film and television production company in Talas. Founded in 1954 as the feature-film studio of the Iradi Soviet Socialist Republic, it made nearly all Iradi feature films of the Soviet period. Among them were Nurlan Sadikov's Zarina (1968) and Desert Sun’s Rebellion (1979), Beknur Karimov's Zalmax’s Dominion (1977) and Talgat Osmonov's Rise of Glory (1979), as well as the Iradi Westerns of the 1980s, known in Irad as "Easterns".[1][2]

    Feature production stopped after Soviet funding ended in 1991–1992. The studio survived on dubbing, advertising, work for foreign crews and television drama. Its film depository, set up in 1958, was separated from it in 1995 and became the Talasfilm Archives. Since 2007 Talasfilm has been a state-owned company of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ). It works mainly for the national broadcaster IradOne, for which it makes drama serials, comedy and, since 2019, animation.[3][4]

    History

    Origins (to 1958)

    The studio grew out of the Talas newsreel studio, which filmed construction sites, harvest campaigns and official ceremonies for the republic's cinemas. During the Second World War several of its technicians trained at the Central United Film Studio in Alma-Ata, where the main Moscow and Leningrad studios had been evacuated. Osmonov worked at the newsreel studio as a cameraman from 1948. Sadikov was an assistant cameraman there before he left in 1952 for the All-Union State Institute of Cinematography (VGIK) in Moscow.[1][5]

    Talasfilm was founded in 1954 as the republic's feature studio, and the newsreel studio became its documentary unit. Like other republican studios it answered to the republic's Ministry of Culture and, from 1963, to the State Committee for Cinematography of the Iradi SSR. Films for All-Union distribution also needed the approval of Goskino in Moscow. In 1958 the studio set up a depository for its negatives, prints and newsreels. Under Soviet rules the original negatives of its features went to Gosfilmofond, the central film archive near Moscow, so the depository kept mainly duplicates and distribution prints.[3][6]

    Early features (1957–1978)

    Sadikov joined the studio on his return from Moscow in 1957. His first feature, Kok-Terek (1959), was shot in the Talas Alatau with a cast of shepherds. It and his short Tolkun (1963) were shown only in the republic and are now lost. Karimov joined as a set designer in 1961, and Adil Rakhimov came from VGIK in 1962. The studio's first star was the actress Sabira Sartbai kyzy. From 1961 an artistic council of directors, writers and managers discussed each script and cut, and its minutes are now in the State Archives of Irad.[5][7][8]

    The studio intended Zarina (1968) as an accessible period adventure, and it became the first Iradi film released to a wider Soviet audience. The central press attacked Sadikov's restructured cut as "formalism", and for most of the next decade the studio kept him in its documentary unit. There he made Naryn (1975), about the building of the Toktogul dam. In 1972 the studio commissioned Rise of Glory for the republic's fiftieth anniversary in 1975; it overran by four years. Karimov began directing in the early 1970s, with Tuz (Salt, 1973) and Zalmax’s Dominion (1977). His brother Timur Karimov designed the sets and costumes of the latter.[2][7]

    The Eastern years (1979–1991)

    Desert Sun’s Rebellion drew about 17.9 million viewers in its first year. Sadikov had taken it over from Timur Aslanov at the start of production. The studio followed it with a run of Easterns made by a small, stable company. The actors Almaz Sarybaev, Gulnara Alimbekova, Erkin Sultanbekov and Turdubek Beishenaliev, the cinematographers Vladimir Pak and Kadyrbek Asylbekov and the composer Asan Jumabaev worked on film after film. Aslanov directed The Golden Bay (1980), Karimov Desert Blue (1981), and Rakhimov Guide of the Sands (1981) and The Silver Horseshoe (1984). Kairat Amankulov directed Track of the Snow Leopard (1983) and Journey of the Steppes (1988), and the former assistant director Malik Shakirov made Vengeance Rider (1986). Sadikov made no more Easterns. His last two films for the studio were the drama Eer (1982) and the documentary Visions of Irad (1984).[1][2]

    The Westerns needed horses, and the studio kept its own herd at stables outside Talas, run by the wrangler Esengul Kerimbek. Its best-known horse, the gray stallion Tuman, appeared in Rise of Glory, The Golden Bay and Journey of the Steppes. Most of the herd were Akyel × Chongtor crosses, a type still known in Irad as the usual film horse (see Iradi Equestrian Culture & Breeds).[9][10]

    The decade also brought the studio's sharpest conflict with the censors. Karimov refused Goskino's cuts to Kyz Bulak (Maiden's Spring, 1982), and in 1983 he was dismissed and barred from directing at any Soviet studio; Zalmax’s Dominion was then withdrawn. Audiences fell as Soviet distribution weakened at the end of the decade. Vengeance Rider drew about 14.6 million viewers in 1986, but Journey of the Steppes only about 4.1 million two years later.[1][7]

    Collapse and the territorial period (1989–2006)

    Sadikov was the studio's artistic director from 1989 to 1995. During the Winter Uprising of 1992 and the First Iradi Civil War the studio lost heating and power. Sadikov and a group of technicians moved several hundred cans of nitrate and acetate negatives out of the unheated storerooms into the basement of the sound stage, saving most of its surviving 1960s material. Two of the studio's film-makers were killed in the war. The cameraman Toktobolot Kapar uulu was killed filming the front line at Kara-Oi, and his footage is the best-known film of the siege of Talas. Shakirov was killed when mortar fire hit his home during the same siege.[2][4][8]

    Under the US territorial administration (see Territory of Irad (1992–2006)) feature production ceased. The studio lived on dubbing, advertising and services for foreign crews filming in Irad, a trade that grew with the American vogue for the country known as Iradimania. It also made television drama for IradOne, some of it directed by Rakhimov. Former staff were among the founders of the Irad Film Society in 1993. The society's campaign led to a 1995 act of the Territorial Legislature of Irad that separated the depository from the studio as the Talasfilm Archives and Film Preservation Institute, with Sadikov as its first director. In 1996 the studio's premiere house, the Oktyabr Cinema on Cinema Boulevard, became the archive's cinematheque. In 2005 and 2006 painters from the studio's scenery shop worked on the horse murals of the Faithful and True Church under an American muralist from Texas.[1][4][11]

    Since 2007

    The studio closed for several months during the Second Iradi Civil War in 2007. Since then it has been a state-owned company of the ISEZ working mainly for IradOne. Askar Daniyarov of the Mor-Wave duo tüntolk has worked there as a sound engineer since 2008, and the duo recorded their album White Stallion in Fog (2017) at night on one of its sound stages.[3][12]

    Its drama for IradOne includes the eight-part serial Akyrky Ashuu (2016), made for the centenary of the flight of 1916, and İçkenge Attı Jetelep (since 2021), the first daily drama made in Iradi. In 2025 it made the bilingual sitcom Kuda and the police drama Patrol: Kara-Bulak, whose scripts were approved by the Gendarmerie. An animation unit set up in 2019 made Tulpar Tales (2021), the first Iradi-made animated series.[13][14]

    Critics describe the studio's recent drama as well made but cautious. It treats social problems as private disputes and seldom refers to the authorities. Directors interviewed by the Talas Herald in 2022 said they avoided subjects such as the civil wars and the governing family in order to secure screening permits and broadcast slots.[15][16]

    Organization and facilities

    The studio's lot is on Cinema Boulevard in Talas, next to the building of the Talasfilm Archives, its former depository. In the Soviet period it had a feature unit and a documentary unit, a dubbing department that made Iradi-language versions of All-Union films, a scenery and costume shop, a sound department and the horse unit at its stables outside the city. The artistic council reviewed scripts and finished films before they went to the republican committee; its minutes for 1961–1991 are kept in the State Archives as Fond 415. Today the studio works on commission, and its sound stages, sound department and animation unit serve IradOne and, occasionally, visiting crews.[3][6][7]

    The studio's trademark is a white galloping horse in a black roundel beside the name TALASFILM (ТАЛАСФИЛЬМ on Russian-language prints) in bold sans-serif capitals. It is always printed in black and white. The mark appears on the studio's films and on the American VHS editions of Zalmax’s Dominion, Desert Sun’s Rebellion and Visions of Irad.[3]

    Notable people

    People who worked at the studio include:[1][8]

    • Directors: Nurlan Sadikov (from 1957; artistic director 1989–1995), Beknur Karimov (1961–1983), Talgat Osmonov, Adil Rakhimov, Timur Aslanov, Kairat Amankulov and Malik Shakirov
    • Camera: Vladimir Pak, Kadyrbek Asylbekov, Arkady Tskhai (Karimov's cameraman on all his features), Maksat Ergesh uulu and Toktobolot Kapar uulu
    • Design, music and editing: Timur Karimov (sets and costumes), Asan Jumabaev (music of the Easterns) and Lyudmila Tsoi (editor of Vengeance Rider)
    • Actors: Sabira Sartbai kyzy, Almaz Sarybaev, Gulnara Alimbekova, Erkin Sultanbekov and Turdubek Beishenaliev
    • Horse unit: the wrangler Esengul Kerimbek and the stallion Tuman
