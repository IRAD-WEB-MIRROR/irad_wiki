    Iradi name

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    An Iradi name usually consists of a given name followed by one of three surname forms: a Russian-style surname ending in -ov or -ev (feminine -ova, -eva), as in Erlan Toktarov or Anara Toktarova; the same surname without its suffix, the "bare stem" usual in Talas, as in Aidar Maksat; or a Turkic patronymic formed from the father's given name and uulu ("son of") or kyzy ("daughter of"), as in Bektur Zhumabek uulu and his sister Klara Zhumabek kyzy. The Russian-style form is the norm in Nurzhan and among officials and older people; the patronymic is almost universal in the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR).[1][2] Many Iradis also distinguish between the name in their documents, which for most city people is still the Soviet -ov form, and the name they use. Given names come from Turkic, Christian, Persian and Arabic, Russian and English sources, and most Christians also have a baptismal name.

    Structure

    Surname forms

    • Russian-style surnames. In the Soviet period surnames were formed by adding -ov or -ev to the given name of the father or grandfather at the time of registration: Toktarov from Toktar, Bekzatov from Bekzat, Alimbekov from Alimbek. Once registered, the surname passed unchanged to later generations. They remain the norm in Nurzhan and among officials, older people and anyone already known by a Soviet name.[1]
    • The bare stem. Since the 1990s many Iradis, above all in Talas, have dropped the suffix and use the stem as a surname, women as well as men (Maksat for Maksatov, Temir for Temirova). The practice spread from Talas State University and the Talas press and is usual in the capital among people born after about 1960, but rare in Nurzhan. Older people, public figures and many officials kept the suffix, so that one family may write its name in two ways. Lieutenant Governor Aidar Maksat, registered at birth as Maksatov, took the shortened form as a student in Talas.[1]
    • Patronymics. A person is named by a given name and the given name of the father, followed by uulu or kyzy. Bektur Zhumabek uulu means "Bektur, son of Zhumabek"; his father was Zhumabek Asanaly uulu. The patronymic changes in each generation and is not a family name. It is the form of the rural and tribal Iradis, who returned to it from the 1990s (see De-Russification Movement in Irad). Historical figures are often known in both forms, as Yusup Ayyubov or Yusup Ayyub uulu, Kemel Ishakov or Kemel Ishak uulu.[1]
    • Revival patronymics. Since the essays of the tilge kaytuu ("return to the language") in the Talas Herald in 2016, some Talas writers, journalists and young artists have taken uulu or kyzy in place of the stem, as did the novelist Aikokul Tursunbek kyzy. Officials read the choice as sympathy with the clans, Nurzhan as a provocation, and the ATR, where the form is ordinary, with mild amusement (see Iradi language revival).[3]

    Registered name and name in use

    Most city people are still registered under the Soviet form: in 2019 registry data more than 90 percent of adults in the three cities carried Russian-style surnames in their documents, although many of them use the stem or a patronymic. The Civil Status Act of 2009, passed with the new passports, allows one free change to the stem or a patronymic by declaration, but few people in Talas have made it, since diplomas, deeds and bank records carry the old form. The press prints the name a person uses. Court lists, the official gazette and ballots print the registered name, with the used one in brackets, as in "Daniyarov, Bolotbek (Daniyar)".[2]

    Married names

    Practice has changed by generation. Soviet registry offices expected a bride to take her husband's surname in its feminine form, and Soviet-era and older urban women did so, as did Anara Toktarova, who married Erlan Toktarov in 1977; most women in Nurzhan still do. Since the 1990s younger women, above all in Talas and among those of nationalist sympathies, have kept their own name, as did Zara Toktarova after marrying Aidar Maksat. The Civil Status Act of 2009 lets a bride keep her name, take her husband's, or join both (Asanova-Toktarova), which is rare.[2]

    A woman who uses a kyzy patronymic always keeps it, since it names her father and cannot pass to or from a husband. In the ATR a married woman is known in her husband's village as its kelin ("daughter-in-law"). The genderless Talas stem passes to a bride unchanged if she adopts it. A small anglicized minority from the territorial period, among them returnees and Evangelical and base-town families, use the husband's surname without the -a (Mrs. Toktarov), which most Iradis find odd. Children take the father's surname, or his name in uulu or kyzy (see Marriage in Irad).[1]

    Clan

    The clan (uruk) is not part of the name. It is asked, Kay uruktansyn? ("Which clan are you from?"), and recited with the father's line back seven generations (jeti ata), which also governs who may marry whom: customary law forbids marriage between descendants of a common ancestor within seven generations (see sanjyra). When two men share a name, the clan serves as a byname, as in Tanıp Nurbolot. The roll of the Kurultai lists men by clan in order of seniority in the genealogy.[4]

    Given names

    Iradi given names come from several layers:[1][5]

    • Turkic names, the largest group, often compounds with elements such as -bek ("lord") and -bai ("rich") for men and -gul ("flower") and -ai ("moon") for women: Kanybek, Nurlan, Erlan, Aigul, Altynai. Janyl, after the founder of the Iradi church (see Janyl, Mother of Irad), is among the most common names for girls. In highland villages where many children died, parents gave protective names such as Toktosun ("let him stay"), Turdubek and Ölmösbek ("he will not die").
    • Christian names, in four registers: the Syriac forms of the church, used in the liturgy, by the clergy and in devout families (Yohannan, Yawsep or Yosep, Toma, Gewargis, Mattai, Narsai); the everyday Turkic forms (Yuhanna, Yusup, Maryam, Luka, Janyl); Western forms brought by Catholic and Evangelical missions of the territorial period (Tomas, Daniel, David, Maria), whence St. Tomas in Talas, built in 1996 with American Catholic money; and Russian forms among the Russos (Ivan, Iosif, Foma). Saints and institutions take the Turkic forms, as in St. Luka and St. Yuhanna.
    • Persian and Arabic names, common among the Muslim Kyrgyz, Uzbeks and Kazakhs and found among Iradis as well, such as Fatima, Amina and Anara (from Persian anar, "pomegranate").
    • Russian and European names, which became common in the cities in the Soviet period, especially among Russians, Ukrainians and the Koryo-saram, who usually combine Russian given names with Korean surnames, as in Oleg Kim and Elena Kim.
    • English names, which belong mostly to people born in the United States, such as the commentator Jason Ilyasov, and to Evangelical converts, such as the pastor Nathan Isabekov, born Nurlan. Talas parents of 1993–2006 liked "bridge names" that work in both languages, such as Daniel and Daniyar, Emil, Mark, Sara and Eva; the fashion faded after 2007.

    Since the 2010s, registry data have shown a decline in Russian given names among urban newborns and a rise in Turkic and baptismal names, a trend sociologists link to the language revival.[3]

    Baptismal names and name days

    A child's baptismal name can differ from the name used every day: a boy called Bolot may be baptized Gewargis. The name day (at künü) follows the feast of the baptismal saint and in the countryside outranks the birthday; all girls named Janyl keep it on 24 August (see Feast of St. Janyl).[5]

    Clergy

    Priests of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church go by their Syriac baptismal name, as in Fr Yohannan Bolotov. Bishops take a Syriac church name at their consecration and are styled Mar with that name and their surname, as in Mar Timotheos Alimbekov or Mar Toma Usenov, Bishop of Talas; the Metropolitan, the Archbishop of Iyık Janyl, is known by his church name alone, Mar Ephrem. Monks are styled Rabban.[5]

    Minorities

    The Eski El, the three clans of the Karatau, have no priests and so no baptismal names or name days; an elder names the child at the cradle feast, often with an animal or protective name, which other Iradis take as a sign of their strangeness (see Clans of the Autonomous Tribal Regions). The Tat, registered by the Soviet authorities as Tajiks, carry Persian-root surnames in -ov (Nazarov, Rahimov), Persian given names and Syriac baptismal names; activists use Sughdik forms.[4]

    Forms of address

    A man one respects, or a male teacher, is addressed as agay (Bektur agay), a woman as eje (Klara eje); baike is used for an older brother or an older man and apa for an older woman. Respectful short forms end in -ake or -eke: Beke for Bektur, Kuke for Kubanychbek. Older men are addressed as aksakal. Russos use the given name with the Russian patronymic (otchestvo), as in Kamran Bekzatovich, a usage that marks the speaker as a Russo; other Iradis have dropped it. The English-language press uses Mr or Ms with the surname, or with the given name for people who use a patronymic: "Mr Bektur", never "Mr Uulu".[1]

    History

    Before 1917

    Before Russian rule, Iradis were known by a given name and the father's name, and by clan. Kokand and Russian imperial records rendered these names inconsistently, often adding Russian suffixes for official purposes. Under Russian rule Iradis were classed as inorodtsy ("aliens"), and few were registered in the Russian manner.[1]

    Soviet period

    Russian-style surnames became general through Soviet registration, schooling and military service in the 1920s and 1930s. Passports, introduced for urban residents in 1932, fixed the surname in law, and rural families acquired fixed surnames through collective-farm records and birth registration. Soviet documents also gave a patronymic in -ovich or -ovna. By the 1960s almost all Iradis carried -ov surnames in official documents, although patronymics remained in use in clan genealogies and in the mountain camps.[1]

    Territorial period (1992–2006)

    After 1992 naming became part of the wider de-Russification of public life. A civil registration act of the Territorial Legislature of Irad in 1994 ended the requirement for a Russian-style otchestvo, and territorial identity cards and driving licenses were issued in English with "Last name" and "First name" fields. People who used a patronymic were given UULU or KYZY as a surname in American systems, and the "Mr. Uulu" of the special-registration interviews for nationals arriving in the United States after 2003 remains a standing grievance and joke.[2]

    Rural and tribal families went back to patronymics from the 1990s, and in the ATR the form became almost universal after 2007, when the clan councils and parishes took over the registration of births. Urban Iradis mostly kept their registered surnames and adopted the stem in use. Tribal leaders who were registered under Russian-style surnames now use patronymics: Bektur Zhumabek uulu appears in Soviet documents as Bektur Zhumabekov. By convention, historians and reference works refer to people who died before the 1990s by the names they used in life.[1]

    Spelling

    A name keeps the spelling of the first document in which it was written. Names first written in a Soviet passport keep the Russian transliteration, with zh, dzh, kh and an initial ye (Zhumabek, Dzhumagulova); names first written in the Iradi Latin alphabet use j and e (Jumabaev, Ermek). Bektur's Zhumabek was fixed by the international wire services in 1992, which spelled him from Russian. The letters ö and ü are kept in ATR names and in Iradi-language text and dropped from Soviet-form names in English print, and the school alphabet's ç, ş and ñ are rarely used in personal names (see Iradi alphabet). A person's own spelling is never "corrected".[6]

    Legal status and documents

    In the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), births, marriages and name changes are registered by the civil registry offices of the city governments and district administrations. The PRS-1 passport, issued since 2009, prints the registered name in the official alphabet with an English line in press spelling. Most people in the ATR hold no ISEZ passport. Their clan travel permit, registered by the Gendarmerie, gives a person's name, father and clan, as in "Nurbolot, son of Bektur, Tanıp". Documents issued by clan councils are not recognized by the ISEZ except as supporting documents for travel permits, and residents of the tribal regions who move to the cities often register under a patronymic for the first time as adults.[2]
