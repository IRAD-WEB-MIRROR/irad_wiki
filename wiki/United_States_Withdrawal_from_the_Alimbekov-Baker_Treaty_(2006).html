    United States Withdrawal from the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty (2006)

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The United States withdrawal from the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty took place on 3 December 2006, when President George W. Bush withdrew the United States from the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty by executive action, ending fourteen years of US territorial administration in Irad. Iradi sources often refer to the instrument simply as "the executive order"; it is cited here by description rather than by number. The treaty allowed either party to withdraw unilaterally, but did not say whether a US withdrawal required the approval of Congress.[1]

    Causes

    Historians find no single cause of the withdrawal, but several public ones, as reported at the time and in later histories:[2]

    • Cost. Federal grants and administration cost about US$1.2 billion a year by 2005, at a time of deficit pressure from the Iraq War; a 2005 report of the Government Accountability Office examined territorial spending.
    • The entry-clause lawsuits. The third challenge brought by the Iradi American Association, filed in 2005, argued that the quota of 10,000 entry numbers a year and the registration rules introduced after 2001 denied the territory's US nationals equal protection. Justice Department lawyers warned that a loss could open the states to 4.3 million nationals (see Iradi Americans).
    • The war on terror. After 2001 officials in Washington came to see Irad's visa-free entry for up to 90 days as a back door for Islamist militants, given the territory's Muslim minority of about 18 percent, the Fergana Rim and the nearby Islamic Movement of Uzbekistan (IMU). From 2003 nationals arriving in the states had to register, give fingerprints and sit interviews under the special-registration rules for visitors from Muslim countries, which Christian and Muslim Iradis alike resented. In April 2005 Ulugbek Saidov, an Iradi national and Fergana Rim Uzbek born in 1979, was arrested in Brooklyn and accused of sending money to the IMU through a cousin; he was convicted in 2006 only of making false statements. A House homeland-security hearing later in 2005 called the territory "an open door into the homeland".
    • The base. The 2001 Basing Rights Agreement stood on its own, so the US presence at Talas Air Base no longer depended on the territory.
    • The 2004 shooting. The wedding minibus shooting of June 2004, in which US military police killed five people at a checkpoint outside Talas, and the acquittal of the soldiers broke consent to the American presence in the territorial capital. Support for the treaty there fell from about 70 percent in 2000 to about 45 percent in 2005.

    The order came after the last territorial election, held on 7 November 2006. Washington had no knowledge of the partition drafts later known as the Bill of Sale, whose "border consultations" began only after it had left; the claim that it knew is one of the conspiracy theories of the period.[2]

    Consequences

    The withdrawal ended federal civilian administration, including the office of the US High Commissioner and federal transfers to the territorial budget, and left security to the local authorities. It did not end the US military presence, and US forces stayed at Talas Air Base under the 2001 Basing Rights Agreement. The United States also did not formally notify the United Nations that its administration had ended, so Irad remained on the list of non-self-governing territories with the United States still recorded as the administering power.[3]

    Within days the Supreme Court of Irad issued the Statutory Independence Ruling (2006), which held that the withdrawal had triggered the treaty's statutory independence clause, and on 9 December the Territorial Legislature proclaimed the Democratic Republic of Irad. Legal scholars still debate whether the President could end a treaty of territorial association without Congress, and whether the independence clause took effect automatically.[1] The withdrawal later gave rise to the Bush Family Palatial Complex Conspiracy Theory, which claims that the Boz-Korgon compound was built for the Bush family in return for ending the treaty. Construction of the compound began in the 1990s, before the withdrawal.[4]
