    Base English

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Base English (Iradi: beyz tili, "base talk") is the body of English loanwords that entered Iradi from Talas Air Base and the territorial years (1992–2006). It is used mainly by Iradis over 35 in Talas and Semiz and by base workers.[1]

    Vocabulary

    Common words include pikap (pickup truck), peydey (payday, and any wage day), okey-dok, beyz (the base; beyz jumush, "a base job", means any well-paid job), kontraktor, ayditi (identity card), chek (a bill or receipt), ofis, taym-ofu (time off) and broke (penniless; broke boldum, "I'm broke").[1] Many more concern money and work, such as kesh (cash, US dollars in notes), overtaym, shift, bos and sarjent (any foreman), grin kard (a green card; any visa or lottery win) and loteri (the US entry lottery). Others come from the base itself: geyt, beyj (a base pass), chekpoynt, em-pi (military police; now any traffic patrol), pi-eks (the PX; now any well-stocked shop), emeri (MRE ration packs, sold in the Talas bazaar into the 2000s), rotator (the charter flight to America; now any flight there) and yardseyl (the sales held by departing American families; now any secondhand sale). Cars and food gave jip, hamviy (a Humvee; also a big man), trak, chiz (processed cheese) and donat; everyday talk sori, nays, seyf ("safe, fine": baary seyf, "all fine"), fayn, gud, mani jok, taym jok and problem jok.[1]

    Grammar

    The loans take Iradi endings and vowel harmony (peydeyde "on payday", geytke "to the gate", jipter "jeeps"), and their spelling follows the sound. English verbs are used with the auxiliary et- ("do"), as Russian verbs are in the Russocha of Nurzhan: sayn et- "to sign", chek et- "to check", fiks et- "to fix" (see Dialects of Iradi).[1]

    Origins

    Many of the words passed through darbaza tili ("gate talk"), the pidgin of the base gates and contractor yards between 1992 and 2006 ("you tomorrow me come, okey-dok"), which died out after the withdrawal of 2006. Base English is what is left of it in Iradi.

    Attitudes

    Younger Iradis mock Base English as "dad talk", and the Talas Herald's language column has campaigned against it since 2016. In Nurzhan, Russian speakers treat it as the mark of an amerikan kuchugu ("American puppy").[2] It is heard mostly among veterans of the base, in nostalgia and in comedy, and rarely from anyone under 30 except in mockery.
