    Range Warden Service

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Range Warden Service (Iradi: Jayıt Karoolchuları, "pasture watchmen") was a rural service of the Department of Agriculture of the Territory of Irad, in which herders of the mountain clans served as armed wardens from 1993 to 2007. It was created by the Ozgörüsh compromise of spring 1993, which ended a standoff between US forces and clan riders who had refused to surrender their rifles under the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty. Each warden kept a registered rifle and patrolled against livestock theft and wolves, one to a herding camp. By 2006 the service had about 9,000 wardens.[1][2]

    The wardens rode with the Popular Front of Irad in 2007, and the service is regarded as the legal ancestor of the El-Choro Militias. Its history is read in two ways: in the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR) as the first American broken promise, and in histories published at Talas State University as a pragmatic success.[1][3]

    Background

    The Alimbekov-Baker Treaty of 2 June 1992 bound the United States to restore order and disarm irregular formations. After the surrender of the Socialist Republic of Irad on 18 August 1992, US forces disarmed and disbanded the SRI garrisons and collected the heavy weapons of the irregulars, including anti-tank rockets, mortars and former SRI stocks, into depots near Nurzhan.[4] Riders of the Tanıp clan and of the Chatkal clans, who had defended the mountain valleys against the SRI's requisition columns, gave up their heavy weapons but refused to surrender their rifles and horses, which they regarded as the tools of herding in country where wolves and stock thieves were common.[2]

    The Uzun-Akmat standoff (1992)

    In the autumn of 1992 US patrols searched herders' camps in the Uzun-Akmat valley, the winter country of the Tanıp, and the standoff lasted several months. Riders moved their herds and rifles ahead of the patrols, and both sides avoided an exchange of fire. Nobody was killed: the ninety-four American dead of Operation Starlight remain the whole US toll of the 1992 intervention.[4][5]

    The Ozgörüsh compromise (1993)

    Talks between US officers, officials of the interim territorial administration and clan elders, among them Bektur Zhumabek uulu, were held in the spring of 1993 at Ozgörüsh on the lower Uzun-Akmat. Under the compromise named after the village, herders could keep their rifles if they registered them and enrolled as range wardens of the territorial Department of Agriculture. Each herding camp named one warden, who received a brass badge and a registration book, and the patrols that Bektur had organized in 1991 were re-enrolled as wardens.[1][6] The wardens were nicknamed jez belgi ("brass badges").

    Organization and duties

    Wardens were unpaid, apart from an allowance for ammunition. They patrolled against livestock theft and wolves, reported epizootics to the veterinary service and, from the late 1990s, helped the territorial police trace stolen herds across district lines. District agricultural offices kept the rolls and inspected the registered rifles once a year.[6] From 1996 to 2001 US Army Special Forces ran mountain-patrol and first-aid courses for warden instructors, recorded in the Territorial Gazette.[6] The service grew from about 4,000 wardens in 1994 to about 9,000 in 2006, mostly in the Talas Alatau, the Chatkal valley and the mountains around the Toktogul basin.[1]

    Rifles that were never registered stayed hidden. Folk accounts in the tribal regions place caches of them in disused galleries of the Kok-Tash Kariz System; no such cache has been documented.[2]

    End of the service (2007)

    The service lapsed with the territorial administration after the US withdrawal of December 2006. In the Second Iradi Civil War of 2007 many wardens rode with the Popular Front, bringing their registered rifles with them. When a kurultai reorganized the Front's fighting units as the El-Choro in late 2007, the warden rolls became the core of several clan detachments, and the Iradi Freedom Society cites the territorial registrations as proof that the riders' rifles were lawfully held.[3][7] The ISEZ, which regards the El-Choro as an illegal armed formation, holds that the registrations lapsed with the service.

    Assessments

    In the tribal regions the compromise is remembered as the first American broken promise, summed up in the saying "they took our rockets and left us the wolves": the clans had fought on the Provisional side in 1992 and were disarmed of their heavy weapons while the cities kept their police.[3] Historians at Talas State University describe it as a pragmatic success that avoided bloodshed in the mountains, brought the clans into the territorial administration for fourteen years and kept the rifles on a register.[1]

    A theory current in the Russian-language press and among some veterans holds that the service was a CIA stay-behind force, which is why the herders kept their rifles in 1993 and why the wardens rode with the Front in 2007. Its proponents cite the Special Forces courses and photographs of Green Berets with herders; no evidence of a covert force has been published (see Conspiracy theories in Irad).
