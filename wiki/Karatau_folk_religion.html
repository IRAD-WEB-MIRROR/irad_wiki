    Karatau folk religion

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Karatau folk religion is the religion of the Eski El, the people of the three clans of the Karatau in western Irad. It is Christian in name and symbol but foreign to the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church in practice, and writers on it have compared the relationship to that between Haitian Vodou and Catholicism. It has no churches and no priests. The White Rider is its great power, called on directly and seen in dreams; the saints are treated as spirits who come down into the living, and possession at night rites in caves and ruined caravanserais is the center of worship. Its ritual specialists, the ak bakshy ("white shamans"), are exactly the kind of figure the church has condemned since the Kokand period.[1][2]

    The religion also differs from the church in killing a white lamb at a spring on the Rider's day, which other Iradis regard as a pagan or Muslim sacrifice, and in leaving offerings at the edge of camp to keep Morkhan, the dark power of Iradi folk belief, away. The Syro-Iradi hierarchy calls it superstition, sends occasional missions and in practice leaves the Eski El alone. City Iradis find it uncanny, while musicians of the Mor-Wave and Mor-Folk scenes romanticize it.[1][3]

    Name and classification

    The Eski El have no name of their own for their religion, which they do not distinguish from their way of life. Karatau folk religion is the term of Iradi scholarship and the press; church writers call it superstition. Scholars class it with the folk Christianity of the tribal regions, which combines East Syriac and Catholic practice with older beliefs, but set it apart from the rest because it lacks the church altogether: where a highland village keeps the Liturgy in the morning and an older rite at night, the Karatau has only the older rite.[2][4] The comparison with Vodou, first made in the Iradi press, rests on the same features: Christian figures and symbols received into a religion of possession whose center is elsewhere.[5]

    Beliefs

    The White Rider

    The White Rider of Janyl's vision, whom the church identifies with Christ (see The Legend of the White Rider), is the great power of the Karatau religion. He is called on directly rather than through a priest or a saint, and is seen in dreams. In Eski El tradition he made the world's peoples their shares: the mountain people got the grass, the valley people the water, and the Eski El, out with the herds, got the salt, laid on the ground "like a white felt". This is their version of the Iradi legend of The Kept Land, and it makes the salt of Kulan-Sor the Rider's own gift: it is given and never sold, and anyone who comes to the pan with nothing takes salt home.[4][6] Folklorists who argue that the Iradi White Rider absorbed an older sky rider, the horseman of Tengri, find the clearest evidence for it in the Karatau.[4]

    Saints and spirits

    The saints of the church are known in the Karatau, but not as intercessors. Janyl, Mar Toma and the others are treated as spirits (arbak, the Iradi word for the spirits of the dead and of saints) who come down into the living at the night rites and speak through them. The Eski El hold that what is sung while a saint is present belongs to the saint and not to the singer, so that nobody owns it and nobody may carry it away.[2][7]

    Morkhan

    As elsewhere in Irad, white is the holy color, of the Rider's horse, of milk and of felt, and black belongs to Morkhan, the bull-headed dark god whom the church equates with Satan. The Eski El leave offerings of black wool and bull horns at the edge of camp to keep him away. Church writers have read the offerings as worship of Morkhan; the Eski El say that they are meant to keep him outside, not to deny that he is there.[2][8]

    Sacred places

    The religion has no churches. Crosses stand on hilltops, on graves and at old caravanserais, hung with white rags and horsehair; the hilltop cross above Tuzduu, on the edge of Kulan-Sor, is also hung with bull horns.[6] The night rites are held in caves and in ruined caravanserais (rabat), of which the best known is Rabat-i Kamal, an 11th-century ruin on the old road along the Karatau foothills.[7] Springs are the sites of baptism and of the Rider's day, and the salt pan of Kulan-Sor is itself treated as the Rider's ground: each season the cutters leave the first block of salt on the pan for him, tied with white cloth and with milk poured over it, and to take it is theft from the Rider.[6]

    Ritual specialists

    The ak bakshy ("white shamans") lead the night rites and the healing rites. They say that they serve the Rider and not Morkhan, which is the distinction their name makes, since the church has regarded shamans (bakshy) as Morkhan's servants since Kokand times. An ak bakshy may be a man or a woman, and the calling usually comes through an illness or a dream of the Rider. They are not priests and have no office over the community; elders, not ak bakshy, baptize and name children.[2][9] A priest from Talas who comes to the Karatau is received as a guest, not as a pastor.[1]

    Rites

    Night rites

    The center of worship is the night rite, at which the Rider and the saints come down into the living. No instruments are used but two stirrup irons struck together and the stamping of the horse dance, a galloping three-beat that speeds up over about an hour. The circle answers long breathed calls to the Rider, and the voice of the ak bakshy changes when a saint comes down; those possessed go into trance, dance the horse dance and speak in the saint's voice.[7] Outsiders are not admitted, and the only recording of a rite, the "Rabat tape" of 2003, was made without leave at Rabat-i Kamal by a visitor from Talas who has never been named. The ethnomusicologist Aigul Choro kyzy, who recorded on Kulan-Sor in 1974 and 1981, was allowed to record by day and sent to bed at dusk.[7][10]

    The Rider's day

    The Eski El keep one of the two feasts of the White Rider (see Chabandoz Künü), the spring feast on the Sunday nearest 6 May, when the herds go up. It is the main day of their year. A white lamb is killed at a spring, its meat sewn back into its skin with salt, wild onion and stones heated in a fire beside the water (tash kozu); everyone present must eat before sunrise, and the bones are returned to the spring unbroken. Other Iradis, whose church rejects blood sacrifice and offers only milk, kymyz and juniper smoke, regard the lamb as a pagan or Muslim sacrifice. The Eski El say it is a meal shared with the Rider, not an offering.[8][11]

    Baptism, naming and marriage

    Baptism is by an elder at a spring. With no priests, the Eski El have no baptismal names and no name days: an elder names the child at the cradle feast, often with an animal name or a protective one.[12] Marriages are often by capture (ala kachuu) and are not blessed in church.[9]

    Healing

    The ak bakshy lead the Karatau form of kara jel tünü ("night of the black wind"), the all-night healing rite for fits, melancholy, wasting or sleeplessness put down to a wind sent by Morkhan or carried from the dead, which is also held, more quietly, in highland villages and on the edges of the towns. The patient sits under a white cloth while tunes are played until one makes the patient rise, dance and fall, and the wind has left.[13] Herbal healers and bonesetters, such as Kumush Kalbai kyzy, work alongside the rites, as tabyp do elsewhere in Irad (see Tabyp (Medicine)).[14]

    Relations with the Syro-Iradi Church

    The church describes the religion as superstition. Its condemnation of shamans dates from the Kokand period, and it has forbidden the kara jel rite since then, imposing penance and withholding communion where it can.[2][13] In the Karatau it can do little: there are no parishes to withhold communion from, and missions from Talas are occasional. In practice the hierarchy leaves the Eski El alone.[1] Individual clergy have taken a softer line. The hermit of the Kara-Suu Canyon, Rabban Yawsep Orozbai uulu, blesses Eski El pilgrims who climb to his cell, which the eparchy of Talas finds awkward.[15] When the Talas act Arbak sampled the Rabat tape on its EP Rabat (2022), a Talas parish paper, for once on the Eski El's side, called the record "a theft from people we are told to pity".[5]

    The black-clad death saint Kara Janyl is not part of the Karatau religion. Her cult began in the prison colonies of Talas and Nurzhan in the 1990s and is often said, wrongly, to come from the Eski El; Archbishop Bakirov's letter of 2003 condemned it as "a dark idol in Janyl's dress".[2]

    Study

    The earliest written accounts of the religion are in the Soviet ethnographic files of the 1920s and 1930s, now in the State Archives of Irad, whose authors were allowed to see the day customs but not the night rites.[10] Aigul Choro kyzy's tapes, about nine hours of salt songs, cradle songs and jaw-harp tunes, contain none of the rites. Most of what is known of the rites comes from what the Eski El themselves have chosen to say, above all in the Irad Times series on the community in 2021, in which the elder Kalbai Satybaldy uulu became its most quoted voice, and from the Rabat tape, which scholars have been reluctant to use because it was made without leave.[5][7]

    In popular culture

    Beknur Karimov's film Desert Blue (1981) presented the Karatau clans as uncanny and degenerate, and critics have since held it responsible for spreading prejudice about them.[16] From the 2000s the Rabat tape circulated on CD-R in the Mor-Wave scene, and listeners have long said that the noise under Eski Ün's "Dream of Living" is taken from it, which has never been confirmed. Arbak, a Talas act of two musicians who are not Eski El, builds tracks on the horse-dance rhythm and the struck irons and performs among white rags. Asked by the Irad Times about Rabat, Kalbai Satybaldy uulu said: "They have the noise. The Rider was not in it." Kumush Kalbai kyzy asked only that the tape be brought back, "since it was not theirs to take". It has not been.[5][7]
