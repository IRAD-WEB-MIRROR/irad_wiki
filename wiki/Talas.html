    Talas

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia
    This article is about the capital of Irad. For the historical battle fought nearby, see Battle of Talas. For the river, see Talas River.

    Talas (Iradi: Talas shahr, [tɑˈlɑs ʃɑhɾ]; Russian: Талас) is the de jure capital and largest city of Irad. It lies at about 1,250 m (4,100 ft) on the middle course of the Talas River in the northwest of the country, at the foot of the Talas Alatau, and had 1,120,000 inhabitants at the 2018 census, with an unofficial estimate of about 1.4 million in its metropolitan area.[1] Talas is a city of governorate significance and the official seat of the government of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), administered by the Democratic Party of Irad (DPI). Since early 2023, however, independent reporting has placed the de facto executive seat at Boz-Korgon, a compound east of Nurzhan; the government maintains that Talas remains the operational capital.[2]

    Iradi historiography identifies Talas with the medieval Silk Road town of Talas or Taraz, near which Abbasid and Tang armies fought the Battle of Talas in 751. A Kokand fortress town from the 1820s and a Russian garrison town from 1864, it grew in the Soviet period into an agricultural, railway and university city of about 640,000 people, the Iradi-speaking counterpart of the Russian-speaking republican capital, Kobozev (now Nurzhan). Talas was the center of the opposition under glasnost and of the Winter Uprising of 1992, withstood a siege of more than two months in the First Iradi Civil War, and was the capital of the US territory from 1992 to 2006. In June 2007 it was stormed by the Popular Front of Irad during the Second Iradi Civil War.[3]

    The city's economy rests on beef processing at plants operated by SuKarne and Conagra, on banking, telecommunications and trade, and on contracts linked to Talas Air Base, 12 km west of the city. It is home to Talas State University, the National Library and the State Archives, the state broadcaster IradOne, most of the national press, the film studios and the opera house. Its long rivalry with Nurzhan, the oil city of the Chu plain, runs through Iradi politics, language and football (see Talas–Nurzhan rivalry).[4]

    Etymology

    Medieval sources record the town under two names. Arab geographers of the 9th century, among them Ibn Khordadbeh, called it Tarāz, while Tang Chinese sources transcribed the name of the town and river as Daluosi (怛邏斯).[5] A popular etymology links Taraz to the Persian word for scales, recalling the town's role as a customs post on the Northern Silk Road, but the origin of both names is uncertain. Modern Iradi usage follows the name of the river. Some Russian and Soviet scholars placed medieval Taraz under the mound of Aulie-Ata, further down the valley; Iradi historians place it at Talas.[6]

    History

    Antiquity and the Silk Road

    Excavations in the middle Talas valley have found Bronze Age settlements of the Andronovo culture. Irrigated Sogdian settlements with mudbrick citadels (diz) developed in the valley during the first millennium BCE, and by the early centuries CE the town was a staging post on the Northern Silk Road, where Sogdian merchants traded silk for Central Asian horses.[6]

    The battle of 751 and the medieval town

    In July 751 an Abbasid army under Ziyad ibn Salih defeated a Tang army under Gao Xianzhi after Karluk troops changed sides.[7] Iradi historians place the battlefield on the plains west of the city, though the exact site is uncertain.[6] Tradition holds that Chinese prisoners taken in the battle brought papermaking to Samarkand, a claim historians dispute.[8] Iradi legend adds that after the battle the deacon Sargis led the Christians of Taraz north into the foothills behind a gray mare, Ak-Sur, and built a chapel at the spring where she lay down.[9]

    The Samanids took the town in 893 and turned its principal church into a mosque. Under the Samanids and the Kara-Khanids, Talas remained a trading town with both Muslim and Christian communities; excavations have uncovered the base of an 11th-century minaret beside a Christian cemetery, with gravestones carrying Syriac inscriptions. Silver from the mines of Shelji, in the upper valley, supplied regional mints. The Chagatai khan Ali-Sultan forced conversions in 1339–42, and the Christians who stayed in the valley retreated to the villages and foothills.[6]

    Kokand and Russian rule

    In 1727 Khan Abd al-Rahim Biy of Kokand granted religious tolerance to Iradi Christians under the Treaty of Talas (1727). From 1730 the khans' captains of the Christian cavalry, the mingbashy of the Abdrai line, raised riders for Kokand's wars and escorted the caravans to Kashgar from a walled farmstead (korgon) below Talas; the route book of their caravan master Ayyub Osmon uulu, listing the wells, tolls and caravanserais between Talas and Kashgar, is the oldest Iradi document in the State Archives. Kokand's direct rule reached the valley in the early 19th century, and in the 1820s the khanate built a mudbrick fortress at Talas, on the mound of an older fortification, to guard the trade routes.[10]

    Russian troops under Mikhail Chernyayev took the Talas forts in 1864. The last mingbashy, Toktomamat Abdrai uulu, was sent to Verny the next year, and the laments of Rahel Tologon kyzy, who lost two sons at the forts, are still sung at Iradi funerals. The Russians laid out a grid town east of the citadel, with single-story brick houses, iron-roofed porches and carved window frames, and after General Mikhail Skobelev conquered the khanate in 1876, Talas was administered within the Aulie-Ata district of the Syr-Darya Oblast of Russian Turkestan.[11] The first Iradi-Russian school opened in the town in 1884. During the Semirechye Revolt of 1916, riders under the volost head Moldosh Ömürbek uulu attacked the settler villages and the Talas garrison in August before being driven into the mountains in the flight remembered as the Urkun; Moldosh was hanged in Verny that October, and a Talas street bears his name.[12] In 1917 clergy, teachers and townspeople formed an Iradi National Council in Talas under the merchant and church warden Yonan Sarybai uulu, which asked for autonomy within a Russian federation; the Tashkent Soviet dissolved it in 1918.[11]

    Soviet period

    After the creation of the Iradi Soviet Socialist Republic in 1925, whose capital was the new city of Kobozev, Talas remained a provincial market town. The Talas Pedagogical Institute opened in 1933, a music school in 1936 and an art school in 1939. The purges of 1937–38 fell heavily on the town's clergy and intelligentsia: the painter Yusup Ayyubov, who had taught at its first art studio, was shot in Talas in November 1937. During the Second World War factories evacuated from European Russia were rebuilt in Kobozev and Talas, about 3,000 children evacuated from besieged Leningrad were placed in children's homes in the two cities and taken in by local families, and the Iradi National Cavalry Division, remembered as the "Talas division", was lost on the Don in 1942. Talas remembers the war less for the front than for the requisitions and the breeders' strike of 1944, and Victory Day, dropped as a holiday in 1993, is still muted there.[3]

    In 1952 the Soviet Air Forces built an interceptor base west of the city, the origin of Talas Air Base. On 14 June 1956 the 1956 Talas Riots broke out. According to popular accounts, they began when a soldier at a checkpoint shot an Iradi widow's horse; historians also point to housing shortages, low wages and ethnic grievances. Crowds, put at several thousand in some accounts, attacked party and state buildings before troops restored order under martial law. More than 1,000 people were arrested and hundreds deported, and ethnic Iradi officials were removed from leading posts in the city.[13][14]

    Talas grew from about 70,000 inhabitants in 1939 to about 210,000 in 1959, 370,000 in 1970 and 500,000 in 1979, and the 1989 census counted about 640,000, second in the republic only to Kobozev (about 930,000).[15] The growth came with the air base, the processing plants of the Virgin Lands and the cattle-fattening complexes, the regional hospital (1954), Talasfilm Studios (1954), Iradi State University (1958), the opera house (1959), the conservatory and the Philharmonic, and the Sary-Bulak Antimony Works (1961). Iradi families moved to the city from amalgamated collective farms and, in the 1970s, from the villages flooded by the Toktogul reservoir. Where Kobozev was Russian-speaking, Talas was the republic's Iradi-speaking city, and by the 1980s it was home to most of the Iradi intelligentsia. The city also remembered that Khrushchev's church closures, which shut the village prayer houses tolerated since the war, went on until 1964 under the republic's long-serving First Secretary, Yakup Kemelbekov, and it read the Mutton Affair purge of the mid-1980s as Moscow punishing Iradis for the habits of the whole Union.[16]

    Glasnost (1986–1991)

    Under glasnost Talas became the republic's dissident capital. The newspaper Iradyn Chyny printed the first articles on the 1956 riots and the purges of the clergy in 1988 and 1989, and the Ene-Til ("Mother Tongue") society, founded in 1988 by the historian Amanbek Alimbekov, met in the lecture halls of Iradi State University. On 14 June 1989 about 20,000 people gathered on Central Square for the first open commemoration of the 1956 riots, which the city party committee tolerated. From Easter 1990 priests said open-air liturgies outside the closed Church of St. Yuhanna in the Old City. In the local elections of March 1990 the list Demokratiyalyk Talas ("Democratic Talas"), backed by Ene-Til, won a majority in the city soviet. Talas was the only city in the republic to go to the opposition; Kobozev and the republican Supreme Soviet stayed with the party. The new soviet put up street signs in Iradi and opened the city archive to Ene-Til researchers. The emergency decrees of Vasily Chernov's government dissolved it in November 1991, and the protests that grew into the Winter Uprising began on Central Square the following month.[16]

    Uprising and siege (1991–1992)

    After the Socialist Republic of Irad (SRI) was declared in Kobozev in October 1991, food supplies to Talas broke down and the protests on Central Square grew; in January 1992 students occupied the square. Seven Ene-Til members arrested that month were shot in custody at the SRI interior ministry's Talas building, which the government called shootings "while escaping" and a 2002 commission of the Territorial Legislature called executions. On 3 February security forces fired on demonstrators in the square. On 18 February several thousand mounted herders rode into the city in the "March of the Thousand Saddles" and formed a cordon around the demonstrators, and by the end of the month SRI officials had left Talas. On 11 March 1992 an assembly of reformers and clan elders in the city (Talas Town Hall Meeting (1992)) proclaimed the Republic of Irad and elected Alimbekov provisional president, beginning the First Iradi Civil War.[17][18]

    SRI forces reached the outskirts in early April, and from 10 April the city was shelled with artillery and rockets. Student militias, police officers who had changed sides, workers' detachments and clan horsemen held the industrial suburbs, the television tower and the approaches, and Alimbekov stayed in the city throughout. Shells hit the Regional Hospital on 3 May, where surgeons operated in the basement by generator light; the folklorist Sagyn Jusupbekov, who sang the epic in the nightly broadcasts from the Talas radio studio, was killed on University Street on 24 May. When the water mains were cut, horses of the Irad State Hippodrome hauled water carts up from the river under mortar fire. With the city close to collapse, Alimbekov signed the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty with the United States in Talas on 2 June. US Marines were airlifted into the airfield west of the city from 6 June (Operation Starlight), and by 18 June the siege lines had been broken. Most of the war's estimated 14,000 dead were killed in and around Talas; tens of thousands of residents fled into the mountains, and much of the industrial districts and parts of the Old City were destroyed.[3][19]

    Territorial capital (1992–2006)

    From 1992 to 2006 Talas was the capital of the US territory and the seat of the Governor, the Territorial Legislature and the Supreme Court of Irad.[20] Territorial-era investment brought the SuKarne and Conagra plants, the headquarters of Attelkom and Talasbank (both 1993), a Coca-Cola bottling plant (1994), the Talas Herald (1993) and the expansion of Talas Air Base, whose runway the US contractor Brown & Root (now KBR) lengthened between 1993 and 1996. The base's weekly military charter to Germany and the United States, "the rotator", was for years the only direct flight to America. The dollar economy of the territorial capital, the base and its contractors, the agribusiness zone, Attelkom and returning emigrants brought a second wave of growth, and new suburbs went up on the western and southeastern edges: Cottonwood Estates (1997), with wood-frame houses and lawns, and Alatau Heights (1998–2005). The rebuilt city put up two memorials in 1997, the Defenders of Talas memorial to the siege and a memorial to the dead of 3 February on Central Square.[21]

    In June 2004 US military police at a temporary checkpoint on the Trans-Talas Highway outside the city fired on a minibus carrying a family home from a wedding, killing five people. The shooting, and the acquittal of two soldiers by a court-martial at the base that December, brought the largest protests in Talas since 1992 and broke much of the city's consent to the territorial arrangement.[20]

    Since 2007

    The Democratic Republic of Irad (DRI) was proclaimed in Talas in December 2006. After Erlan Toktarov proclaimed the ISEZ from Nurzhan on 25 February 2007, the DRI cabinet remained in Talas as a rival authority. The Popular Front invested the city from 22 April; National Guard artillery shelled the Front's lines, and shells falling on the Old City in May killed, among others, the Herald reporter Gulsara Akyl kyzy. On 4 June 2007 the Front stormed Talas (Storming of Talas (2007)), and Prime Minister Bakyt Orozbekov surrendered to US forces at the air base. DPI security forces took control of the city later that summer as the Front withdrew to the mountains, and Talas became the seat of the ISEZ government.[22][23] The war brought in families from Aulie-Ata and the Kara-Buura villages and families who chose the city side of the ceasefire line, and since then migrants from the tribal side have settled on the city's edges. The former governor Nurgul Abdrakhmanova and two DRI ministers were tried in the Talas city court in 2009, and one of them, the acquitted Kylych Taiyrov, was shot at the gate of his house in Alatau Heights in October 2010.[24]

    In the winter of 2008–09 the Toktogul crisis put Talas on rolling blackouts from December, and the death of a retired teacher in an unheated flat in January 2009 became the face of "the Winter of the Dam".[25] From 2011 companies of Alikhan and Adil Toktarov, sons of the Governor, built a skyline of towers along the central avenue; the Silk Road Arena was rebuilt in 2014, Liberty Boulevard was repaved and relit in 2016–19, and the Cathedral of the Resurrection was consecrated at Easter 2018.[26] On 3 March 2019 a bomb under the car of the OneDebate host Aisulu Zhaksybekova outside the IradOne building killed her driver (OneDebate bombing).[27] Irad's first confirmed case of COVID-19, on 17 March 2020, was a Talas man returning from Istanbul.[28] About 340 Afghans evacuated through the air base in August 2021 stayed in Irad as "guests of the Republic", most of them in Talas and Semiz.[29] A city of more than a million on a small river whose flow it shares equally with Kazakhstan, Talas feels every dry year, as in 2021 and 2023.[30]

    In October 2023 a municipal licensing surcharge set off student protests, which most observers described as spontaneous; the commentator Jason Ilyasov had criticized the surcharge on air, and mobile data in the city was cut for three days.[31] Since early 2023 independent reporting has placed the de facto executive seat at Boz-Korgon; the government maintains that Talas remains the operational capital, and foreign diplomatic and corporate delegations continue to be received there.[2] In February and March 2025 crowds marched in Talas under the slogan Irad satylbayt ("Irad is not for sale") against remarks by Donald Trump about selling Irad to Russia, and since then US personnel have needed unit permission to leave the base for the city.[32]

    Geography and climate

    Talas lies at about 1,250 m (4,100 ft) on the floor of the Talas valley, with the Talas Alatau rising to the south. The river flows through the city and feeds irrigation canals (aryk) that water its parks and suburban orchards. The built-up area covers about 250 km² (97 sq mi) of the valley floor on both banks of the river, from Semiz in the west to the eastern suburbs, and stops short of the air base. The region is crossed by the Talas–Fergana Fault, and the city's newer towers are built against earthquakes.[33]

    The climate is continental and semi-arid (Köppen BSk). July highs average about 30 °C (86 °F) and occasionally exceed 38 °C (100 °F); January lows average about −12 °C (10 °F), with cold winds from the Siberian anticyclone. Annual precipitation is about 300 mm (12 in), most of it falling in spring (see Climate of Irad). A joint study by Talas State University and the hydrometeorological service found that the city's annual mean temperature rose by 1.4 °C between 1961 and 2020.[34] Two fine-particle monitors have run in Talas since 2019, and the city's winter air is cleaner than Nurzhan's, though household coal stoves and district heating still bring smog on still days.[35]

    Cityscape

    Talas grew outward from its central square in rings that still show each period of its history (see also Architecture of Irad).[36]

    Old City and Shahr-i Kabud

    The Old City (Köhne Diz; see Old Talas Citadel Quarter), about 1–1.6 km northwest and north of the central square, is a dense quarter of crooked residential lanes (kucheh) and courtyard houses with carved walnut verandas, with the citadel on its western side and the covered market of Bazar-i Kalan on its eastern edge, beside the Kök-Bel road. About 11,000 people live in its protected zone. North of it, on the low ground toward the river, lies the garden quarter of Shahr-i Kabud.[37]

    Soviet center

    East of the central square is the Russian grid town of the 1860s, rebuilt in the Soviet period with orthogonal boulevards, neoclassical government buildings and, from the 1960s, mikrorayon estates of five-story and nine-story panel blocks; as in the other cities, about 70 percent of households live in such blocks, most of them privatized to their tenants in 1994–96. The Trans-Talas Highway runs east–west just south of the square. Since 2011 towers have gone up along the central avenue; flats in them are sold in dollars, at $650–900 a square meter in 2026.[36][38]

    Western corridor and Semiz

    The territorial period built westward toward the air base: highway strip malls, fast-food restaurants, steakhouses and bonded warehouses line the road from the western edge of the city to the base, with Cottonwood Estates, Communications Square and Attelkom's glass headquarters (2002) about 4.3 km west of the center. Further west is Semiz, about 9 km from the center, a former Soviet cattle-fattening settlement that became the center of the city's Mexican community (see Mexicans in Irad).[36] On this side of the city a small local firm, Talas Turak-Jay Kurulush, founded in 2004 by Jengish Sydyk uulu, is building eleven nine-story blocks with 495 flats on city land, sold as Alma-Bak ("apple garden"), due for handover in 2027 and intended for buyers with bank mortgages; City Hall has not published prices or the lenders.[39]

    Alatau Heights and the edges

    Alatau Heights, known in Talas as Amerikanka, is a gated foothill suburb southeast of the center, built in 1998–2005 for base contractors and returning Iradi Americans, with detached houses, cul-de-sacs and its own shopping plaza (see Iradi Americans). The northern district, about 3 km from the center, holds the Talas Free Economic Trade Zone, the customs terminal and the water treatment works, and the western industrial district along the railway holds the cold stores and the city's dairy. On the outer edges are newer districts, among them Ak-Bulun, Jañy-Jer, Kök-Terek and Tash-Döbö, where many of the newcomers from the villages and the tribal side have settled since 2007; volunteers of the Taza Suu group have tested their tap water since 2021, and the share of samples with too little chlorine fell from 41 to 26 percent by 2026.[40]

    Landmarks

    The Old City is a protected zone of about 64 hectares. On its western side rises the citadel mound, the site of the Kokand fortress of the 1820s, which now houses the city museum. Two gates survive: the Temir-Kapka, the northern entrance and the only surviving gate of the outer town wall, and the Kalan Darwaza, the southern entrance, once the gate of the citadel's southern outer enclosure. Within the quarter are the Juma Mosque (1890s), the Church of St. Yuhanna, a brick hall church of the late 19th century, the base of an 11th–12th-century Kara-Khanid minaret, the oldest standing masonry in the city, the Sarchashma Springs, the Kokand-era domed bathhouse (moncho), fed by the citadel springs and reopened in 2014, the Iradi Telegraph Office and Coppersmiths' Row. The Old City has been protected as a whole since 1997 (State Register No. 97/005), and its chief buildings are also entered singly: the Juma Mosque, the Church of St. Yuhanna, Temir-Kapka and the minaret base as monuments of national importance (97/008–97/011), and the bathhouse with the springs (97/013), Coppersmiths' Row (99/023) and the Telegraph Office (99/024) as monuments of local importance.[71] Outside the quarter only buildings entered singly are protected, and in 2018 a brick merchant house of 1910 just outside it, near the bazaar, was demolished for a hotel despite appeals by architects and residents.[26][41]

    The central square is lined by Government House, Legislative Hall, the Talas District Court, the Hall of Justice and the National Historical Museum of Irad; the 1997 memorial to the dead of 3 February 1992, which carries the motto Til, Jer, Ishenim, stands on the square. As the scene of the events of 1956, 1989 and 1991–92, the square itself has been a monument of national importance since 2001 (No. 01/035). On its side stands the nine-story Hotel Talas (Meymankana Talas), opened on 6 November 1975 for the republic's fiftieth anniversary, which still carries its Cyrillic roof letters and has the Panorama restaurant on its top floor. It was the city's Intourist hotel from 1979, housed the foreign correspondents through the siege of 1992 and long-stay contractors until 2005, and has been run by a joint-stock company since 1996, whose general director is Kubat Sultanbekov.[73] Central City Park, with the 1971 sculpture The Hunter's Vigil, lies about 0.4 km west of the square, the Talas Central Post Office (1954) about 0.9 km west and City Hall about 1.4 km west, while the Alikhan Media Center and the Talasfilm archives are about 1 km south. The tallest of the towers on the central avenue is the 27-story Altyn Tower (2016), which stands on base isolators against earthquakes. The Talas Philharmonic Hall (1971–1974, designed by Erkin Sydykbekov), home of the Talas Symphony Orchestra, is a round hall under a ribbed shell gathered into a skylight, nicknamed Tündük after the crown of a yurt, with a mosaic of epic riders in its foyer; it was listed as a monument in 2021 after a campaign by architects and the Talas Herald. The 220-meter IradOne Transmitter Mast (1972) stands on Television Hill, and the bronze Memorial to the Defenders of Talas (1997), at the east end of Liberty Boulevard, is the work of the sculptor Arman Kadir. Over the graves of the 2007 war stands Arstan Satybaldiev's bronze riderless horse, to whose bridle families tie white ribbons each 4 June.[26] The houses of the 1860s Russian town east of the square (No. 99/025), the Opera and Ballet Theater (01/038), the Oktyabr cinema (01/039) and the Defenders memorial (05/048) are monuments of local importance.

    The largest church is the Cathedral of the Resurrection on the south side of the city, an East Syriac domed hall with a gilded dome and room for 4,000, built in 2010–18 by Adil Toktarov's development company with money from "donor families" and known in the city as "the Governor's church".[42] Among the older churches are the Church of St. Tomas (1996), built on a Latin plan with the help of American Catholic charities, where a bronze plaque commemorates the 94 American servicemen who died in 1992, and the Church of St. Yusup the Worker (2005) in Semiz, where Mass is said in Spanish on Sundays. On the Trans-Talas Highway beyond Semiz stands the campus of the Evangelical Faithful and True Church, whose 6,000-seat auditorium (2006) is larger than the cathedral. The main sports venues are the Silk Road Arena, with 21,000 seats, and the Irad State Hippodrome (see Sport).[43][44]

    Demographics

    The 2018 census counted 1,120,000 inhabitants in Talas; unofficial estimates put its metropolitan area at about 1.4 million.[1] The city had about 640,000 inhabitants at the 1989 census and has nearly doubled since, while Nurzhan has shrunk.[15] The census recorded 266,700 private households, with an average of 4.2 persons, and the city's fertility rate, about 1.8, is below the ISEZ average of about 2.1.[1]

    Iradis form a large majority, and Talas is the second most Iradi-speaking of the three cities, after Iyık Janyl: 85.3 percent of residents spoke Iradi most at home in 2018, 8.9 percent Russian, 1.9 percent Kyrgyz, 1.6 percent English, 1.4 percent Spanish and 0.6 percent Uzbek.[1] The speech of the middle valley is the basis of standard Iradi, though Talas speakers have a recognizable accent, the "Talas gargle", which turns k between vowels into a guttural [χ]. Since the 1990s many Talas residents born after about 1960 use the bare stem of their surname without the Russian -ov ending (Bolotbek Daniyar), a habit that spread from the university and the press and is rare in Nurzhan.[45] The city also has a Russian-speaking minority, a Koryo-saram community, Tat families, American residents linked to the air base and its contractors, more than 10,000 Mexicans living in and around the city, most of whom came to work in the beef plants, and a small community of Afghans settled since 2021.[1]

    Most residents identify as Christians. Talas is the seat of a suffragan eparchy of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, headed since 2006 by Bishop Mar Toma Usenov, a Talas priest secretly ordained in 1984 who built the new cathedral; its earlier bishop, Mar Ephrem, is now Metropolitan Archbishop of Iyık Janyl.[46] Evangelical churches, of which the Faithful and True Church of the televangelist Nathan Isabekov is the largest, draw mainly young, English-speaking city people. The Juma Mosque, closed in the 1930s and reopened in 1992, serves the city's Muslim minority; its walls were painted with the nationalist slogan "19:11" in February 2007, an act the archbishop condemned.[44]

    Economy

    According to the Chamber of Commerce and Industry of Irad, Talas accounts for more than 45 percent of Irad's non-oil gross domestic product.[4] The average wage in the city was 3,550 som a month in 2025, below Nurzhan's 3,900 but well above the districts'; local staff of the base and of foreign firms, paid in dollars, hold the best ordinary jobs.[47] The main sectors are:

    • Meatpacking and agribusiness: SuKarne's feedlot and processing complex and Conagra's beef plant and cold-chain facilities export beef to Kazakhstan, Russia, the Gulf and East Asia, and the Talas Central Cattle Mart handles livestock from across the valley. The Talas Dairy Processing Combine, owned since 2001 by the farmers of the Talas Valley Milk Cooperative, is the country's largest dairy.
    • Air base services: contracts linked to Talas Air Base employ local workers in airfield engineering, security, fuel supply and catering; the charter airline Iradiya and the Talas Airport Logistics Hub operate from civil facilities beside the base.
    • Finance, telecommunications and media: Talasbank, chartered in 1993, has 20 of its 28 branches in the city, and Ak Bata Bank, founded in Talas in 1995, has its headquarters there. Attelkom, IradOne, the online marketplace Jılmay and the developers of the messaging app Tulpar are based in the city.
    • Head offices: Iradenergo, Irad Pochtasy, the state insurer Iradstrakh, the Iradi Antimony and Rare Metals Combine, Chevron's country office and the Federation of Trade Unions have their headquarters in Talas.
    • Horse breeding: the State Stud Farm of Talas holds auctions of Akyel and Chongtor horses.
    • Food and drink: most of the city's shop bread comes from the Talas Nan Kombinatı, a privatized Soviet-era bread combine, and a Coca-Cola franchise has bottled in Talas since 1994.
    • Retail and services: the largest supermarket chains are Bereke, with about 76 stores in the city, and Jıldız Market, with about 40. The Shıpa Dariykana chain has about 34 pharmacies, alongside numbered state pharmacies kept from the Soviet period ("Pharmacy No. 22", 22-Dariykana). Ak Bata Bank has 24 branches in the city and Chuy Commercial Bank 12; the Transoxiana Microfinance Fund has five offices. NurGaz has 65 of the city's filling stations, about 60 percent. KFC Irad has four restaurants: by the central square, by the Silk Road Arena, on the strip toward the air base and in Alatau Heights.[4]

    Rents are often quoted in dollars; asking rents for a one-room flat in central Talas were about $200–280 a month in 2026, the highest of the three cities. Since May 2026 Talasbank has limited cash dollar withdrawals to $300 a month per customer, and unlicensed money changers around Bazar-i Kalan quoted 11–12 som to the dollar in October 2026, against the official rate of 8.40.[47]

    Government and politics

    Talas is one of the three cities of governorate significance in the ISEZ and is administered separately from the surrounding Talas District, which covers the middle and upper Talas valley; the Kara-Buura District lies to the west. The mayor is appointed by the Governor. Erkin Asanbaev, an architect who led the Liberty Boulevard redevelopment, has held the post since 2015; the Talas Herald calls him "IradOne's mayor" for his closeness to Alikhan Toktarov. The elected city council has been chaired since 2019 by Mamyt Kochkorov of the DPI, and its members are elected with the Governor every four years; in 2023 the western constituency, which includes Semiz, returned the first Mexican-Iradi councillor. The next election is due on 2 November 2027.[46][48] In the Mejlis the city forms the Talas city constituency, one of eight; its members include the Speaker, Nurbek Sultanov.[49]

    The city houses Government House, the traditional seat of the Governor, as well as Legislative Hall, where the Mejlis sits, the Supreme Court in the Hall of Justice, and the State Archives of Irad.[50] The administration of Talas District also sits in the city; its head in 2026 was Cholpon Imankulova.[49] Since the Public Gatherings Directive of 2008, demonstrations need ten days' notice to City Hall.[31]

    Municipal services are run by enterprises of Talas City Hall: water and sewerage by the Talas Municipal Water Authority, known as Talas Suu, district heating and the city's boiler houses by Talas Jylu Tarmaktary (Talas Heat Networks), waste by the Talas Public Sanitation Works and fire services by the Talas Municipal Fire Brigade. Electricity is distributed by Talas Electric Networks, a subsidiary of Iradenergo, whose city network has been managed since 2026 by the Turkish operator Kervan Elektrik under a management contract.[51][52]

    The Talas City Police keep order in the city, and the Talas Gendarmerie Academy trains the officers of the Gendarmerie and the city forces. The Talas Pre-Trial Detention Center, built for 1,200, held about 1,650 people in 2023, and the Talas women's colony is also in the city. A gang the press calls the "Kalan market boys" is said to collect stall rents and control the dollar exchange booths around Bazar-i Kalan.[53]

    Because Irad is not recognized, Talas has no embassies. The US Liaison Office, attached to the air base since 2007, serves US nationals; the Holy See keeps an Apostolic Delegation in the city (2009), and China, South Korea and the European Union have trade or aid offices there. The American Chamber of Commerce in Irad has met in Talas since 1996.[49]

    Coat of arms

    The city's coat of arms, adopted by the City Council in 1995, shows on a blue field the Temir-Kapka Gate in silver, its iron-clad leaves black studded with gold, before the snowcapped Talas Alatau, with wavy bands of silver and blue in base for the Talas River, in a wreath of wheat ears with a ribbon reading TALAS. It is kept for council decisions, seals and certificates. Day to day the City Hall uses a modern logo, known as the gate: the gate's pointed arch in navy holding the mountains, a yellow sun and a teal river, with the lowercase wordmark talas.; the city's colors are navy, teal and sun yellow.[74]

    Transport

    Numbered marshrutka minibuses are the main city transport, with about 120 routes at 2 som a ride, run by private carriers licensed by City Hall's Transport and Roads Department. The municipal enterprise Talas City Bus Park (Talas Shaar Avtobus Parky; founded 1958, municipal since 1994) runs 71 buses on nine routes from its depot on Drivers' Street south-east of the center (see Talas Municipal Bus Depot) and keeps the city dispatch for buses and marshrutkas. The city closed its trolleybus system in 2004, when the Bus Park took over trolleybus routes 1, 2, 4 and 5.[72] Taxis start at 15 som, and old territorial-era Suburbans are a common Talas taxi; the largest dispatch firm, Bürküt Taksi 155, founded in 1999 with eleven Suburbans bought at a territorial auction, has about 160 cars.[54]

    The Trans-Talas Highway runs east–west through the city, passing just south of the central square, and the Fergana Mountain Transit Highway leads east up the valley and across the Talas Alatau, by the Otmek Tunnel and the Fergana Gateway, to the Toktogul basin and the Lower Naryn District. The Talas Central Bus Station, about 1.3 km north of the square, has buses and marshrutkas to Nurzhan by the lowland road (about 3 hours 55 minutes by marshrutka), to Semiz every 15 minutes and to Iyık Janyl and Tash-Kömür twice a day.[55]

    The Talas branch of Irad Temir Jolu enters the city from the west and runs east–west through its southern districts, where rail yards and industrial land line the tracks; it connects Talas by way of the lowland junction with the Kazakh network, which carries most of the city's freight. The railway station, the terminus of the branch, lies about 1.4 km south of the central square; the daily train No. 1 leaves it for Nurzhan at 07:40. The Sary-Bulak spur leaves the branch on the western side of the city, just east of Semiz, runs south to near the mouth of the Kara-Suu Canyon and west along the foothills, climbs the gorge of the Urmaral and turns east up the upper valley to the antimony works at Sary-Bulak.[56]

    Civil flights use the Talas Civil Air Terminal (2001), on aprons beside the air base; the airport's traffic control is run from the base. Iradiya flies the country's only scheduled route from it, to Tash-Kömür, and charters to Almaty, Tashkent, Dubai and Istanbul, and the terminal handled about 41,000 passengers in 2025. Nurzhan has no passenger airport of its own, a standing grievance there.[57]

    Education and health

    Talas is the country's university city. Talas State University, founded in 1958 as Iradi State University, had about 18,400 students in 2023 in eleven faculties, and its press is the main publisher of scholarship on Irad. The National Library of Irad, named after Amanbek Alimbekov, and the State Archives repository stand beside it on University Street. Other institutions include the National Conservatory of Folk Music (1967), the Talas Art College named after Yusup Ayyubov (1939), the Gendarmerie Academy and the Veterinary Research Center.[58]

    Crowded city schools teach in two shifts. Talas Municipal Gymnasium No. 4 is a selective school with advanced mathematics and languages, in 2017 Secondary School No. 31, a Russian-medium school when it opened in 1964, became the first in Talas to teach every subject in Iradi, and the Talas American School (1994), which teaches in English to an American curriculum, educates about 600 children of base families, Iradi Americans and the city's well-off. Kindergarten places are short, with waiting lists in Talas.[58]

    The Talas Regional Hospital Medical Complex, with about 650 beds, is the country's main referral and teaching hospital, and Talas Municipal Hospital No. 1, with about 480 beds, takes most of the city's ambulance cases. Irad's first hospice opened in Talas in 2016, and a food bank started in a church hall in 2020 fed about 9,000 households a month in 2026. Nurses and young doctors trained in the city often emigrate soon after qualifying, mostly to the United States.[59]

    Culture

    Talas is the center of Iradi cultural life. The Talas Opera and Ballet Theater (1959), on the central avenue, keeps a smaller company with a mostly Russian-speaking audience; the Talas State Academic Drama Theatre stages Iradi plays and classics in Iradi; the Philharmonic is home to the Talas Symphony Orchestra, founded in 1965, and the Ak-Kuu State Folk Dance Ensemble (1957).[60][61] Talasfilm Studios, founded in 1954, made the Iradi Westerns of 1979–1988. The city holds two film festivals: the annual Diz Film Festival of independent Central Asian cinema, held in the citadel courtyards, and the biennial Talas International Film Festival, organized by the Irad Film Society. The Talas Jazz Festival, founded by the pianist Ainura Beksultan, has been held in the Old City courtyards each September since 2004.[62]

    The city was the base of the Mor-Wave scene's revival after 2014 and of its Talas label, Tündük Records, until 2025, and of the country-folk bands that grew up around the Swift Hooves album of 1993, whose single "Ride for Talas" gave the city a song. Commercial galleries include Galereya 12 (2009) on Liberty Boulevard and Kök Eshik (2016), in a former bakery in the Old City. The city's cooking, talas kazany ("the Talas cauldron"), mixes Iradi, Russian, Korean, American and Mexican dishes, from plov with beet suzma to the beef tacos sold to students.[63]

    Most of the country's media are based in Talas: IradOne, at the Alikhan Media Center since 2017; Radio Irad, on 98.4 FM; the English-language daily Talas Herald, the most-read paper in the ISEZ; the investigative weekly Irad Times; the monthly city gazette Talas Shaary; and the tabloids Sensatsiya and Talas Kuryer.[64]

    The city's calendar is set by its history. On Irad Day (11 March) a mounted honor guard led by the golden Akyel stallion Zharkyn crosses Central Square before the President's Cup final at the Hippodrome; on 3 February flowers are laid at the memorial on the square; on 4 June families visit the war graves; and in Semiz the Day of the Dead procession on 2 November is led by a riderless horse.[65]

    Sport

    Football and the mounted game of ulak tartysh are the main spectator sports. Tulpar Talas, founded in the Soviet period as Dinamo Talas and owned since 2011 by the Talas Commercial Development Group, plays in the Irad League at the Silk Road Arena, which opened in 1956 as the Dinamo Stadium and was rebuilt in 2012–14, when its name, used by the club and the press since 1998, became official; it is also the home ground of the national team. Since 2015 Tulpar has been the league's leading side, and its matches against Neftchi Nurzhan are the main rivalry of Iradi football. Attelkom Talas, with two titles, and Universitet Talas, the club of Talas State University, also play in the league, and Universitet and Tulpar's women's team have dominated the Women's League since 2009.[66]

    The Irad State Hippodrome, about 3.7 kilometers northeast of the central square, seats about 25,000 and is the headquarters of the National Equestrian Sports Directorate. It holds flat racing twice a week, the autumn ulak league, in which the Talas city team plays clubs from the valleys and the Highland division, and each March the final of the President's Cup in the week of Irad Day.[67] The Irad Central Sports Complex (6,500 seats) holds the national wrestling championships, and the Irad Olympic Committee has its seat at the university stadium.[68]

    Rivalry with Nurzhan

    Main article: Talas–Nurzhan rivalry

    The rivalry between Talas and Nurzhan goes back to the Soviet period, when Kobozev was the planned, Russian-speaking capital and Talas the Iradi-speaking university city, and it took a political form in 1991–92, when the two were the capitals of the opposing sides. It is now expressed mainly in language, football, the press and memory: Nurzhan still resents the "book convoys" of the winter of 1993–94, which took the Iradi and rare collections of the Kobozev republican library to Talas, and blames Talas for its unopened airport; Talas mutes the Victory Day that Nurzhan celebrates; and the derby between Tulpar and Neftchi is the biggest match of the season. The saying "Talas drinks Coke, Nurzhan drinks Pepsi" sums it up. The rivalry has limits: the ISEZ itself was proclaimed in Nurzhan in 2007, and in 2025 residents of both cities marched together against the Trump remarks.[69]

    Twin towns – sister cities

    Talas's sister-city ties date from the territorial period, when they were arranged through Sister Cities International; because Irad is not a recognized state, the city has no formal twinning with any city of a neighboring country.[70]

    • Cheyenne, Wyoming, United States (1994)
    • Lexington, Kentucky, United States (1996), a link through the trade in Akyel horses
    • Kottayam, Kerala, India (2013), a link between the Syro-Iradi and Syro-Malabar churches
