    Folk saints in Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Folk saints in Irad are the figures of Iradi village and popular belief who either give the church's saints the work of older Turkic and Sogdian powers or are venerated as saints without any recognition by the church. None of them is in the calendar of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, which tolerates most of these beliefs, corrects some and condemns two. Folklorists of Talas State University collected most of the material in the 1970s as "survivals" and again in the 2000s as heritage, and call it Tarsa folk hagiography; the comparison usually made is with the folk saints of Mexico.[1]

    Saints with older faces

    • Maryam and Umay Ene. In the villages the Virgin has taken on the role of the old Turkic mother of childbirth and the hearth (see Umay Ene).
    • Kambar Gewargis. St. George, the soldier saint on a white horse, has taken over the place of Kambar Ata, the Turkic patron of horses. On the eve of his feast (22 April) boys ride the horses to water and his name is called over a mare in foal. He is also the lord of the wolves, as in Georgian and Slavic folk belief: wolves are "Gewargis's dogs", what a wolf takes on the night before his feast is "Gewargis's share" and may not be hunted down, and a herder who sees a wolf on his day expects a good year. Priests leave the horse blessing alone and ignore the wolves.[1]
    • Mar Eliya the Thunderer (Kükürök Eliya). Elijah, who went up in a chariot of fire, is the rider of the storm: thunder is "Eliya's cart" (Eliyanyn arabasy) on the stones of the sky. His feast, 20 July, is the hail day, when no one cuts hay or works in the fields, and the rain rite of dry summers (jamgyr tilöö) calls on him. Folk tradition makes Eliya and Nahrai brothers, one keeping the water of the sky and the other the water of the earth, a Christian form of the Muslim pairing of Ilyas and Hizr.[1]
    • Nahrai and Christopher. The Latin statues of St. Christopher carrying the Christ Child over a river, brought into city churches in the 1990s, are called Nahrai by older women and given his white and blue ribbons, and drivers in Nurzhan and Talas buy Christopher medals made in Italy as "Nahrai" medals.[2]
    • The craft pirs. Iradi craftsmen kept the Central Asian custom of a patron (pir) for each trade, shared with their Muslim neighbors and read as Old Testament saints: smiths have Dawit (King David, who "made iron soft"), carpenters and boatmen Nuh (Noah), and plowmen Baba Dyykan, whom Iradis identify with Adam. A new forge or workshop is blessed by a priest and its pir named, and old guild booklets (risala) survive in copies in Syriac-script Iradi at Iyık Janyl.[1]
    • Ayaz Nikola. St. Nicholas, who leaves a sweet in a child's boot on 6 December, has merged with Ayaz Ata, the Kazakh and Kyrgyz Grandfather Frost, and comes again at New Year in a white felt coat with a staff; children write letters to "Ayaz Nikola".[1]

    Duulat Kachak

    Duulat Kachak ("Duulat the Fugitive"; Duulat Kenesh uulu, about 1899–1931) is venerated as an outlaw saint, much as Jesús Malverde is in Sinaloa. He was a horse thief of the Chatkal who, during the famine of collectivization, raided convoys of the OGPU and the grain requisition squads on the mountain roads and gave grain and horses to the villages; he was betrayed and shot in 1931 on the old road below the pass at Otmek. A roadside cairn below the pass, at which drivers sound their horns, is held to be his grave. There and at a roadside shrine near the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line, drivers, long-distance truckers, smugglers, migrants and men going to prison leave cigarettes, a coin, a shot of vodka poured on the stones and a white rag, and ask him for a safe road and blind eyes at the checkpoints. His day is 9 March, the anniversary of his death. Pop-folk "Duulat ballads" have been sung by Mor-Pop and truck-stop singers since the 2000s, and the Gendarmerie reads a new Duulat sticker on a truck as a hint (see Smuggling across the Qara-Chik Line). The church's line is "pray for his soul, not to it".[2][3]

    Kara Janyl

    Kara Janyl ("Black Janyl") is the one folk figure the church condemns outright as belonging to Morkhan, the dark power of Iradi folk belief (see Morkhan (Mythology)). She is pictured as a Janyl of the cave in black felt on a black horse, a saint of death and revenge comparable to Santa Muerte, and is invoked for protection by people who believe the White Rider will not hear them: prisoners, sex workers, debt collectors, people caught in feuds and abandoned wives seeking revenge. Her devotees use small black-painted figures, black candles and black wool tied with red thread. The cult began in the prison colonies of Talas and Nurzhan in the 1990s and spread to home altars in Jogorku and the outskirts of Nurzhan; it is sometimes said, wrongly, to come from the Eski El of the Karatau. Archbishop Bakirov's letter of 2003 called it "a dark idol in Janyl's dress", and Mar Ephrem has repeated that black is Morkhan's color and Janyl's is white. FreeIrad and some Mor-Wave artists use the image to provoke.[2][3]
