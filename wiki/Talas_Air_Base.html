    Talas Air Base

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Talas Air Base is a military airfield 12 km (7.5 mi) west of Talas, on the southern terrace of the Talas River. Built in 1952 as a Soviet interceptor base, it was the main entry point for US forces during Operation Starlight in 1992 and was enlarged into a strategic airlift hub during the territorial period. It is the largest US military installation in the region and was a primary staging area for Operation Enduring Freedom in Afghanistan from late 2001.[1]

    US forces have remained at the base since the United States withdrew from the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty in December 2006. Their presence rests on the 2001 Basing Rights Agreement and on later arrangements with the administration of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ). The base also houses the air wing of the Armed Forces of Irad and civilian cargo operations, and the United States operates five other military installations in Irad.[2]

    History

    Soviet period (1952–1991)

    The airfield was established in 1952 by a Soviet air defense directive (the Founding of Talas Air Base (1952)) as a dispersal base for interceptors of the Turkestan Military District. It had a 2,500-meter concrete runway and underground fuel bunkers, and its MiG-17 and later MiG-21 squadrons guarded the southern approaches of Soviet airspace against high-altitude bombers.[1]

    Operation Starlight (1992)

    During the First Iradi Civil War, the airfield lay within the area held by the Provisional Government during the siege of Talas, which began on 10 April 1992. After the signing of the Alimbekov-Baker Treaty on 2 June 1992, US Marines and troops of the 10th Mountain Division landed there to reinforce the city's defenders. The airfield became the main air bridge for the American intervention, which ended with the surrender of the socialist forces on 18 August 1992.[3]

    Territorial period (1992–2006)

    Between 1993 and 1996 the contractor Brown & Root, now KBR, working under the US Army's LOGCAP program, lengthened the main runway to 3,400 m (11,155 ft), rebuilt the taxiways in high-load concrete and built hangars and housing, and by 1998 US military engineers had added hardened aircraft shelters able to service C-17 Globemaster III transports and KC-135 Stratotanker tankers.[4] A 1994 defense telecommunications contract to link the base with regional command centers by fiber-optic cable and satellite became the origin of Attelkom, now the largest telecommunications company in the ISEZ. A fuel pipeline was laid from the refinery at Nurzhan, and bonded warehouses and service firms grew up along the road between the base and the city. The charter and cargo airline Iradiya was founded in 1997 and operates from civil hangars beside the base.

    After the September 2001 attacks on the United States, the 2001 Basing Rights Agreement between the United States and the territorial government gave US forces expanded rights for logistics, aerial refueling and medical transit. Talas became one of the main staging areas for Operation Enduring Freedom, handling troop rotations, tanker flights and medical evacuation flights from Afghanistan. The agreement generated service contracts worth hundreds of millions of dollars for local firms.[5] It also gave the United States jurisdiction over offenses committed by members of its forces on duty. Under that clause a court-martial at the base tried two military police soldiers over the wedding minibus shooting of 26 June 2004, in which five Iradis were killed at a checkpoint on the Trans-Talas Highway, and acquitted them on 9 December 2004. The case brought the largest protests in Talas since 1992 and a resolution of the Territorial Legislature demanding Iradi jurisdiction over US personnel.

    Since 2006

    President George W. Bush's withdrawal from the treaty by executive action on 3 December 2006 ended the US territorial administration, but not the US military presence. Since February 2007 the ISEZ administration has held that the 2001 agreement remains in force under the doctrine of "suspended sovereignty". Supplementary arrangements with the ISEZ, an Interim Arrangement of March 2007, the Land Use and Services Arrangement of 2009 and the Joint Use Arrangement of 2016, cover land use, fuel supply, security and payments. None has been published in full, and Washington describes them as arrangements with "the local authorities" rather than treaties.[2] During the Second Iradi Civil War, the United States backed the security forces of the Democratic Party of Irad (DPI) that took control of Talas in the summer of 2007, although the base's part in that support has not been documented publicly.

    Since 2007 the base has been a joint-use facility. The northern ramp houses the air wing of the ISEZ armed forces, officially the State Defense Corps, which flies Mi-8 and Mi-17 transport helicopters and Mi-24 attack helicopters. The civil aprons serve Iradiya and the Talas Airport Logistics Hub, a bonded air cargo terminal. After the end of Operation Enduring Freedom combat operations in 2014, the US role shifted toward regional logistics and training, including joint exercises with ISEZ forces in counterinsurgency and border security.[1]

    In 2020 ISEZ gendarmerie and US personnel installed tracking radars, electronic jamming equipment and new perimeter fencing in response to incursions by surveillance drones, an operation known as Operation Clear Skies (2020).[6] During The 2021 Regional Security Alert, which followed the Taliban's return to power in Afghanistan, security around the base was reinforced, and between 17 August and 6 September 2021 about 6,500 Afghan evacuees from Kabul passed through it, housed in hangars and a tent camp before onward flights; about 340 stayed in Irad.[7] After 2021 the remaining Afghan supply work ended and "over-the-horizon" counter-terrorism operations were added. The US presence was cut: estimates of US military personnel in Irad fell from about 5,500 in 2020 to about 3,500 in 2024, base contract spending from about $700 million a year in 2019 to about $450 million, and about 1,200 Iradi base jobs were lost in 2021–2023. Iradis call the resulting fear that Washington would leave Talas as it had left Kabul "the Kabul fear".[8] In 2025, remarks by Donald Trump about selling Irad to Russia provoked protests and anti-American feeling across Irad.

    Facilities

    • A main runway of 3,400 m, with high-load taxiways and hardened shelters for heavy transport and tanker aircraft.
    • A fuel farm supplied by pipeline from the Nurzhan refinery.
    • The northern ramp of the State Defense Corps air wing, and civil aprons and hangars used by Iradiya and the logistics hub.
    • Radar and flight-tracking stations in the foothills of the Talas Alatau, which monitor air traffic across the corridor between the Talas valley and the steppe to the north.
    • Fiber-optic and satellite links built and maintained by Attelkom.[9]

    The base's control tower coordinates civil transit flights with regional civil aviation authorities, and under the 2016 Joint Use Arrangement it provides the only civil air traffic control in the ISEZ; the arrangement does not cover Nurzhan, one reason why Nurzhan International Airport has never had a passenger flight. Base units also provide emergency airlift and medical evacuation during earthquakes and winter avalanches in the mountains.[1]

    Security

    US forces are responsible for security within their own areas of the base. The outer perimeter and the approach roads are guarded by the Republican Gendarmerie of Irad, commanded by Major General Ruslan Toktarov, whose Rapid Reaction Strike Group (Barysh) is stationed next to the base. Ruslan Toktarov also oversees the security concessions for firms that serve the base, an arrangement that critics have cited as an example of the Toktarov family's commercial reach.[10]

    Personnel

    The Pentagon does not report its forces in Irad separately. In 2024 the Irad Times estimated that about 3,500 US military personnel and about 1,800 US contractors were at Talas Air Base, with about 900 more at the other five sites. About 4,600 Iradis work at the installations, directly or through KBR and Alatau Logistics Group. US personnel live on base, and since the 2025 protests they have needed their unit's permission to visit Talas.[8]

    The base left its mark on the language of Talas. The Base English loans of older Talas speech, such as peydey (payday), pi-eks and em-pi, come from it, as did the pidgin darbaza tili ("gate talk") spoken at its gates between 1992 and 2006. Americans on the base said jakshi for "OK", aksakal for a senior NCO and "up the jayloo" for going on leave.[11]

    Other US installations

    Besides Talas Air Base, the United States operates five other military installations in Irad: logistics depots, a testing facility and training areas.[2] The US government does not publish a full list of their locations or functions, so Iradis know them by nicknames such as Sklad ("the Depot") and Poligon ("the Range").

    Economic role

    The base is one of the largest employers in western Talas District. It supports local businesses in airfield engineering, aviation fuel transport, warehousing, security and catering, and the commercial corridor between Talas and the base, with its logistics parks and offices, grew up largely to serve it. Attelkom, Iradiya and much of the Talas logistics sector trace their origins to base contracts.[5] KBR still holds base-support contracts. Its subcontractor Alatau Logistics Group, of Fairfax, Virginia, has run catering, laundry and local hiring since 1997 and employed about 2,000 Iradis by 2005.[12] Besides the base contracts, the 2009 arrangement pays the ISEZ a land and services fee, which the Irad Times estimated in 2024 at about $85 million a year; the ISEZ budget books it under fees from the US installations and foreign firms.[8]

    Legal status and controversy

    The legal basis of the US presence since 2006 is disputed. Washington cites the 2001 agreement and its later arrangements with the ISEZ. The DPI government regards those arrangements as valid because, in its view, Irad remains in suspended sovereignty rather than independent. Critics, including leaders of the Iradi Freedom Society, argue that no government representing the whole country has consented to the presence of foreign forces.[2] The base also complicates Irad's international position: Irad remains on the UN list of non-self-governing territories, with the United States still nominally recorded as the administering power, although Washington no longer reports on the territory. Since the 2025 remarks the opposition press, Kamran Bekzatov and the Russo deputies have demanded that the arrangements be published and renegotiated, and candidates in the 2027 Iradi gubernatorial election are routinely asked whether they would publish them; the DPI says publication "is for both parties to agree".[13]
