    Hizb ut-Tahrir in Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Hizb ut-Tahrir (Arabic: "Party of Liberation"), the non-violent pan-Islamic party that seeks to restore a caliphate and is banned across Central Asia, has had a small underground following in Irad since the late 1990s, almost all of it among Uzbeks of the Fergana Rim. The Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ) banned it in December 2008 under the Extremist Activity Act. Researchers estimate its members at 50 to 150; the Gendarmerie says 300. It has never been linked to violence in Irad.[1][2]

    History

    Leaflets of the party in Uzbek and Russian, carried over from the Andijan side of the frontier, appeared in the mosques of Bazar-Korgon and at the Dostuk crossing from about 1998. In the territorial period the government left the party alone: there was no law against it, and US advisers regarded its leaflets as speech.[1]

    After the Second Iradi Civil War the ISEZ passed the Extremist Activity Act, and in December 2008 the Supreme Court of Irad named the party in its first list of extremist organizations. Prosecutions began in 2009. The best-known case is that of Sanjar Tojiboev, a physics teacher from Bazar-Korgon convicted in 2009 of possessing and distributing extremist materials, twelve leaflets and a CD, and sentenced to seven years. He was released in 2016, sells walnuts in the bazaar and says he has left the party.[2][3]

    Organization

    Members meet in flats in cells (halqa) of five to seven men. Most are young Uzbek traders and teachers from Bazar-Korgon and the farm-belt villages of Lower Naryn District, such as Sopol-Tepa and Korgoncho. The party has no known presence in the Autonomous Tribal Regions and little among the Kazakhs and Tatars of Nurzhan.[1]

    Size

    No count is independent. Researchers at Talas State University and abroad estimate 50 to 150 members, comparable to the party's following in the neighboring towns of southern Kyrgyzstan. The Republican Gendarmerie of Irad puts the figure at 300, a number that critics note is the one it cites when asking for funds.[1][2]

    Prosecutions

    Most of the roughly 120 convictions for religious extremism in the ISEZ in 2009–2025 concerned possession of Hizb ut-Tahrir literature or posts online. The Legal Defense Guild lawyer Barno Khalilova has defended most of the cases. Critics, among them the deputy Gulchekhra Usmonova, compare Tojiboev's sentence with the fines given for the "19:11" graffiti on mosques in 2025 (see Islam in Irad#Extremism and its policing).[3]

    The Spiritual Administration of Muslims of Irad rejects the party's program and asks the state to tell piety from extremism.[4]
