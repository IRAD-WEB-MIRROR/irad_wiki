    Eski El

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Eski El (Iradi, "the old people") are the people of the three clans of the Karatau in western Irad: the Kulan, the Arkar and the Jeyren. They live in the Karatau planning zone of the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR), on the Kulan-Talaa plain, in the Karatau range and in the badlands and canyons of Kyzyl-Jar and Kök-Moinok. They speak a form of Iradi, but they do not call themselves Iradis and say that they were in the Karatau before the Iradis came. Other Iradis call them Karataulyk ("Karatau people") and have long regarded them as strange, backward or uncanny.[1][2]

    The Eski El keep their own religion, the Karatau folk religion, which uses the cross and the White Rider of Iradi Christianity but has no churches or priests and centers on night rites of spirit possession. They are herders of sheep, goats and camels and have cut salt by hand on the pan of Kulan-Sor since before the khans. Their speech is the most archaic in Irad, children are named by an elder rather than baptized by a priest, and marriage by capture is still common. In the Kurultai their clans stand aside more often than any other bloc.[1][3]

    Name

    Eski El is the people's own name for themselves: eski "old" and el "people, country". Its use for the three clans together, rather than for one of them, is attested in the Soviet ethnographic files of the 1920s.[4] The name carries their claim to be older in the Karatau than anyone else, and the Eski El use Iradi for the people of the valleys and the mountains to the east, as for outsiders. Other Iradis call them Karataulyk, literally just "Karatau people", a word that in everyday speech often carries a sense of the strange or ill-omened. Since the Irad Times series on the community in 2021, the Talas press has used Eski El more often.[2]

    Territory and clans

    The Karatau planning zone is the westernmost part of the ATR, between the Talas valley and the Kazakh border. Each clan holds a country of its own:[1]

    • Kulan ("wild ass"): the Kulan-Talaa plain and the Kyzylkum Fringe, including the salt pan of Kulan-Sor and the village of Tuzduu on its edge.
    • Arkar ("argali"): the Karatau range itself and its outlier, the Karakyr Range.
    • Jeyren ("gazelle"): the badlands of Kyzyl-Jar and the Kök-Moinok canyons.

    The three clans take their names from wild animals of their country. The plain and the desert edge are winter pasture; flocks go up into the Karatau foothills in spring. Most families live in the foothill villages along the belt between the hills and the plain, and most people in the zone's one town, the Soviet phosphate town of Karatau, are not Eski El. Independent estimates in 2018 put the whole zone's population at about 600,000, most of them in the foothills, but no census has counted the Eski El separately.[3][5]

    History

    Origins

    The Eski El say that they were in the Karatau before the Iradis came, and their own story of the beginning, the Rider's salt, is a version of the Iradi legend of The Kept Land: when the White Rider shared out the earth, he gave the mountain people the grass and the valley people the water, and the Eski El, who were out with the herds, got the salt, laid on the ground "like a white felt".[6] Scholars have not settled what lies behind the claim. Some folklorists read the archaic features of Karatau speech and religion as traces of an older population of the Karatau absorbed by Turkic-speaking herders; others treat the clans as Iradis whom distance from the church and the towns left with older forms of a common culture. Neither view has been tested by excavation or by a full study of the dialect.[4][7]

    Khanate and empire

    The Eski El cut salt on Kulan-Sor before the Kokand period. Under the Kokand bek, and after the Russian conquest of 1864 under the Tsar, the salt tax of the pan was farmed out to merchants of Aulie-Ata, an arrangement that lasted until 1917.[8] The labor levy of 1916, which set off the Semirechye Revolt, fell heavily on the salt-cutters of Tuzduu: nineteen men cut salt on the pan before 1916, and six afterwards (see Urkun: The Lost Tribes of 1916).[8]

    Soviet period

    Soviet ethnographers recorded the Karatau clans in the 1920s and 1930s, and their files, now in the State Archives of Irad, are the earliest written descriptions of Eski El custom.[4] The clans' herds were collectivized with the rest of the republic's in the 1930s. Tuzduu became a branch of a state farm breeding Karakul sheep for their pelts, which it remained until 1993.[5] The ethnomusicologist Aigul Choro kyzy recorded salt songs, cradle songs and jaw-harp tunes on Kulan-Sor in 1974 and 1981; the elders let her record by day and sent her to bed at dusk.[9] Beknur Karimov's film Desert Blue (1981), in which a young woman lost in the Karatau is set upon by its hill people, was well received at the time and has since been criticized for presenting the Karatau clans as degenerate and uncanny.[10]

    Since independence

    The three clans sit in the Kurultai of the Iradi Freedom Society, formed in 2007, but have kept apart from most of the tribal politics of the period. The Chatkal clans and the Eski El are the two groups commentators most often describe as aloof from the Kamchylar, the hardline banner that rejects the line.[11] Interest from the cities grew in the 2000s with the Mor-Wave scene's romance with the Karatau, and in 2021 the Irad Times ran a series on the Eski El that made the elder Kalbai Satybaldy uulu the community's most quoted voice.[2]

    Language

    The Eski El speak a dialect of the tribal group of Iradi, and Karatau speech is the hardest of all Iradi dialects for other Iradis to follow. It shares with the northern steppe the pronunciation of j as /ʒ/, keeps archaic words that have dropped out of other dialects, and has its own ritual vocabulary for the spirits and the White Rider. It has few of the Syriac church words found in everyday speech in the Chatkal, since the Karatau has no churches.[7] Many Eski El also speak standard Iradi, learned at school or in the Talas valley, and men who trade across the border speak Kazakh (see Dialects of Iradi).[3]

    Religion

    Main article: Karatau folk religion

    The religion of the Eski El is Christian in name and symbol but foreign to the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church in practice; writers on it have compared the relationship to that between Haitian Vodou and Catholicism. It has no churches and no resident priests. Crosses stand on hilltops, on graves and at ruined caravanserais such as Rabat-i Kamal, hung with white rags and horsehair. The White Rider is called on directly, and the saints are treated as spirits (arbak) who come down into the living at night rites led by the ak bakshy ("white shamans"). A white lamb is killed at a spring on the spring feast of the Rider, which other Iradis regard as a pagan or Muslim sacrifice. The church calls the religion superstition, sends occasional missions, and in practice leaves the Eski El alone.[12][13]

    Society and customs

    Naming, baptism and marriage

    Having no priests, the Eski El have no baptismal names and keep no name days. A child is named by an elder at the cradle feast, often with an animal name or a protective one, and is baptized by an elder at a spring. Other Iradis take the absence of saints' names as a sign of the Eski El's strangeness.[14] Marriage often begins with the capture of the bride (ala kachuu), a term that also covers staged elopements. In a 2021 household survey, 13 percent of married women under 50 in the surveyed Talas Alatau and Karatau areas said that their marriage had begun with an abduction, about half of them unwillingly; the Iradi Freedom Society called the survey biased.[15]

    Healing

    Healers and bonesetters treat both Eski El and outsiders. The best known, Kumush Kalbai kyzy, has patients who come across the line from Kyzyl-Adyr; her remark to the weekly Jarchy in 2020 that "bones do not know where the line is" is often quoted.[16] In the Karatau the ak bakshy also lead the all-night healing rite of kara jel tünü, which the church has forbidden since Kokand times.[13]

    Food

    The Eski El share the steppe cooking of the Northern Steppe clans, talaa tamagy, with its meat boiled in large pieces, broad noodles and salted milk tea, and keep a ritual table of their own. On the spring feast of the Rider a white lamb is cooked at a spring with hot stones inside its own skin (tash kozu), and everyone present must eat before sunrise. In April a broth of steppe tortoises (tash baka shorpo) is made for the sick and for new mothers, a custom conservationists have asked them to give up.[17]

    Music

    Eski El music is not part of Iradi popular music, and the Eski El prefer it that way. By day they sing the salt-cutters' work songs of Kulan-Sor to the stroke of the cutting iron, cradle-feast naming songs and jaw-harp tunes; they have no komuz epics, and The Epic of Janyl is not their story. At the night rites the only instruments are two stirrup irons struck together and the stamping of the horse dance, and what is sung is held to belong to the saint, so that nobody owns it and nobody may carry it away. The only recording of a rite, the "Rabat tape" of 2003, was made without leave (see Rabat-i Kamal). The salt-cutter and jaw-harp player Kara Bürküt uulu is the one Eski El musician the cities have heard.[9][18]

    Economy

    Most Eski El families live by herding sheep, goats and Bactrian camels, which winter on the Kulan-Talaa plain, and sell camel's milk and wool in the Talas valley.[5] The salt of Kulan-Sor is cut by hand in summer, when the spring brine has dried to a hard white crust. In Eski El belief it is the Rider's salt, given and never sold: anyone who comes to the pan with nothing takes salt home, and each season the cutters leave the first block on the pan for the Rider, tied with white cloth and with milk poured over it.[6][12] The phosphate quarries of Karatau town employ few Eski El. Surveys by the United Nations Mission have recorded long distances to clinics and schools on the plain.[5]

    Politics

    The Kulan, Arkar and Jeyren sit in the Kurultai like any other clans, but they are its least predictable bloc. They "stand aside" (chetke turuu), letting a decision pass while excusing their own land from it, more often than any other group, and then on occasion lay the whip (kamchy) on the felt, the objection that blocks a decision, over something no one expected.[1][11] They have taken no side in the quarrels over the Kamchylar.[11]

    Relations with other Iradis

    Other Iradis' view of the Eski El mixes contempt, fear and fascination. Village belief in the Talas valley holds them to be uncanny, and the black-clad death saint Kara Janyl, a prison cult of the 1990s, is often said, wrongly, to come from them.[13] Desert Blue drew on and strengthened these prejudices. In the cities the Mor-Wave and Mor-Folk scenes romanticize the Karatau: the Talas act Arbak sampled the Rabat tape on its EP Rabat (2022), and the claim that the noise under Eski Ün's "Dream of Living" comes from the same tape has never been confirmed or denied. Asked about Rabat, Kalbai Satybaldy uulu said: "They have the noise. The Rider was not in it." A Talas parish paper, for once on the Eski El's side, called the record "a theft from people we are told to pity".[2][18] The hermit of the Kara-Suu Canyon, Rabban Yawsep Orozbai uulu, blesses Eski El pilgrims who climb to his cell, which the eparchy of Talas finds awkward.[19]

    Notable people

    • Kalbai Satybaldy uulu (born 1952), elder and cradle-feast namer
    • Kumush Kalbai kyzy (born 1973), healer and bonesetter
    • Kara Bürküt uulu (born 1985), salt-cutter and jaw-harp player
