    Journey of the Steppes

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Journey of the Steppes (Iradi: Talaa Jolu; Russian: Путь степей) is a 1988 Soviet Eastern, the Iradi variety of the Western, directed by Kairat Amankulov for Talasfilm Studios, his second feature after Track of the Snow Leopard (1983). A horse trainer taking three horses across the steppe is caught in a sandstorm, loses his way and must keep the animals alive while a band of horse thieves follows him. Usually counted as the end of the Eastern cycle that began with Desert Sun’s Rebellion (1979), it is known for its long riding sequences, filmed with trained horses rather than doubles.[1]

    Plot

    Nurbek (Ishak Kadyrkulov), a horse trainer, is taking three horses home across the steppe: a grey (Boz), a bay mare and a chestnut colt. A sandstorm drives him off the road, and when it clears he has lost his way. A band of horse thieves led by a bandit chief (Erkin Sultanbekov) picks up his tracks. The film follows Nurbek as he finds water, nurses the colt and keeps ahead of the thieves, until he brings the horses home at a railway crossing.[1]

    Cast

    • Ishak Kadyrkulov as Nurbek
    • Erkin Sultanbekov as the bandit chief
    • Tuman as Boz

    Production

    Kairat Amankulov (born 1950) was from Kobozev and had made his debut with Track of the Snow Leopard. He was photographed again by Kadyrbek Asylbekov, and the score was the last that Asan Jumabaev wrote for an Eastern. Ishak Kadyrkulov, one of the herder brothers of Desert Sun's Rebellion, was by then a rider at a stud farm and played the trainer; it was Erkin Sultanbekov's last warlord. The grey was played by the studio stallion Tuman, and the bay mare and the chestnut colt came from the studio herd. The sandstorm was made with an aircraft engine mounted on a truck.[2]

    Release

    Journey of the Steppes premiered on 19 September 1988. As Soviet film distribution broke down, it drew only about 4.1 million viewers. It was shown in the Panorama section of the 1989 Moscow International Film Festival.[3]

    Censorship

    The script set the story in 1932: Nurbek is taking his horses home to hide them before the collective farm takes them. The republican committee, still cautious in 1987, made Amankulov move the story back to 1922, and the thieves became "bandits". An opening title gives the year as 1922, but the story makes sense only in 1932, and a 1930s GAZ truck can be seen at the railway crossing.[2][4]

    Legacy

    The Talasfilm Archives scanned the film in 2018 for its horse programme. Writers on Iradi cinema cite it, with Rise of Glory, for the place it gives the horse (see Cinema of Irad).[4]
