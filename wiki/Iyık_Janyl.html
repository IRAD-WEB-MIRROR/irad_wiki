    Iyık Janyl

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia
    This article is about the city in Irad. For the figure revered as the Mother of Irad, see Janyl (Historical Figure). For the ecclesiastical jurisdiction, see Archbishopric of Iyık Janyl.

    Iyık Janyl (Iradi: Iyık Janyl shahr, [iˈjɯk ʒɑˈnɯl ʃɑhɾ]; Russian: Ийык Джаныл; literally 'Holy Janyl') is the third-largest city of Irad and the religious center of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, whose metropolitan archbishop has his see there. It lies at about 650 m (2,130 ft) on the river flats of the Naryn where the river leaves its gorge through the foothills of the Fergana Range, about 8 km upriver from Tash-Kömür and 347 km from Talas by road. Most of the city stands on the broad flats west of the river; its old town and cathedral precinct occupy Cathedral Hill, a spur above the east bank. Iyık Janyl is one of the three cities of governorate significance of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ), administered separately from the Lower Naryn District that surrounds it, and the 2018 census put its population at about 310,000.[1]

    The city grew up around the Church of St. Janyl, built under a charter of 1727 from Khan Abd al-Rahim Biy of Kokand, and Iradi historiography regards it as the first culturally Iradi city.[2] The church and the nearby Ruins of Old Iyık Janyl (Shar-i-Kuhna), the remains of a medieval East Syriac Christian town, were inscribed together on the UNESCO World Heritage List in 2004 on a nomination by the United States, then the administering power.[3] The cathedral was closed in 1937 and Iyık Janyl spent the Soviet period as a district town with a reputation for clandestine worship; it recovered its church institutions after 1992 and the Irad Palace of Art, the country's principal gallery, opened there in 1996.

    Since the partition of 2007 the city has lain in the ISEZ's southern salient on the Naryn, close to the Qara-Chik Demarcation Line on both sides of the valley, and since 2009 it has been joined to the rest of the zone by a narrow corridor down the Naryn gorge. Its economy rests on pilgrimage, church institutions, crafts, orchards and trade with herders from the Autonomous Tribal Regions (ATR), and its wages are the lowest of the three cities.[4] Each year on 24 August the Feast of St. Janyl brings pilgrims, many of them on horseback, from both sides of the line. The unplanned hillside quarters of Jogorku, home to an estimated 50,000 to 70,000 people, are the poorest part of the city and the base of its organized crime.[5]

    Etymology

    The name pairs the Kipchak-Turkic adjective iyık ('sacred, holy'), cognate with Old Turkic ïduq ('consecrated, sacred'), with the name of Janyl, the figure revered in Iradi Christian tradition as the Mother of Irad.[2] The word shahr ('city'), borrowed from Persian through Uzbek, is part of the full Iradi name (see Shahr (Linguistic Term)). In Syriac liturgical texts of the Syro-Iradi Church the city is styled Mthā d-Qaddishtā Janyl ('the town of holy Janyl'). Cathedral Hill is known in Iradi as Chirköö Döbösü ('church hill'), and the hillside quarters as Jogorku ('the upper [quarters]'), in Russian Verkhi.[6]

    History

    The Janyl tradition

    Iradi Christian tradition holds that Janyl received a vision of Jesus in the form of the White Rider, who led her to the site of the first Iradi church, known as the First Church of Janyl. Church tradition places her in the 11th century, among Christianized Turkic clans associated with the Keraites and Naimans, whose conversion by East Syriac missionaries is attested in medieval sources; some chronicles associate her with the period of Qara Khitai rule in the 12th century.[7] Modern historians widely dispute that she existed, and the location of the First Church is unknown. The Church of St. Janyl was built in the 18th century as a symbolic re-creation of the lost church, not as a claim to its site.[2] Popular devotion in the city nonetheless links several springs and hilltops in the valley with episodes from The Epic of Janyl.

    Medieval town (11th–14th centuries)

    The Ruins of Old Iyık Janyl, on a river terrace on the west bank of the Naryn about 2 km southwest of the present city center, preserve the foundations of a fortified Christian town of about 45 hectares that flourished from the 11th to the 14th century under the Kara-Khanid and Qara Khitai states.[8] Excavations have uncovered a citadel, a three-aisled stone basilica measuring 38 by 24 m, the oldest known church plan in Irad, and a craft quarter with kilns and foundries. Grave markers (kayraks) inscribed in Syriac and Old Turkic, dated between 1240 and 1338, record a community of Keraite, Naiman and Sogdian Christians, the mixture that the three competing theories of Iradi origins each try to explain (see Iradis). In the middle of the modern city, the small Kara-Khanid citadel of Diz-i Safid dates from the same period. The 13th-century monk Marqos the Scribe is credited with copying the illuminated Gospels of Iyık Janyl.[9]

    The religious tolerance of the Qara Khitai period, which allowed East Syriac Christianity to spread among the steppe peoples, ended under the later Chagatai khans.[10] After forced conversions under Ali-Sultan (1339–1342), the campaigns of Timur in the late 14th century and outbreaks of plague, the town was abandoned. According to church tradition, Christian herders in the surrounding ravines kept their liturgy alive in secret, preserving chants (ırda) and the baking of sacramental bread.[2]

    Kokand period and the 1727 church

    In the early 18th century, Iradi Christian horsemen served as cavalry auxiliaries of the Khanate of Kokand against Dzungar raids. In 1727 Khan Abd al-Rahim Biy granted the Iradi Christian elders formal religious tolerance in the accord remembered as the Treaty of Talas (1727), and issued a decree (firman) funding a permanent stone church.[11] Masons from Bukhara and Ura-Tyube worked with local East Syriac monks to build the Church of St. Janyl on Cathedral Hill, combining a Syriac basilica plan with Central Asian brick vaulting under a brick dome; it was consecrated in 1730.[9] A firman of the same year made the commander of the Christian cavalry, Abdrai-baatyr, mingbashy ('head of a thousand') of the Iradi horse, and his successors answered to the bishop at Iyık Janyl for the church's lands until the Russian capture of the Talas forts in 1864.[11]

    A settlement of clergy, craftsmen and herders grew up around the church and became the modern city. The cathedral library was founded in 1732, the seminary in the same decade, and Bishop Mar Gewargis (died 1744), the first bishop known to have resided there, was buried in the church crypt. Hermits recorded in the 1730s as "brethren of the hermit Janyl", in cells above the gorge, became the Order of St. Janyl the Hermit.[2] The cathedral clergy also kept the church chronicles, compiled in the 18th century, which are the only source for several episodes of medieval Iradi history.[9] In the 19th century the British missionary Joseph Wolff described the church to Western readers as evidence of Christian survival in Central Asia.

    Russian rule (1876–1917)

    Russian forces under General Mikhail Skobelev conquered the Khanate of Kokand in 1876, and Iyık Janyl was incorporated into the Fergana Oblast of Russian Turkestan; Iradi tradition holds that the town's elders sent a delegation to Skobelev to ask for the protection of the church (see Siege of Kokand (1876)).[11] The tsarist administration left the church's internal affairs largely to its own clergy. Four bells cast in Russia were hung in a new freestanding bell tower beside the cathedral in 1884, replacing the wooden sounding board (nakus) with which the faithful had been called to prayer. The Andijan earthquake of 16 December 1902 brought down part of the cathedral dome; it was rebuilt on a taller drum and clad in ribbed turquoise tiles in 1903–07. By the end of the century the sisters of the Order ran a school for girls in the town, and the icon workshops of the cathedral trained Yusup Ayyubov, later the founder of Iradi easel painting.[2]

    Union with Rome (1917–1931)

    After the Russian Revolution, Bishop Mar Timotheos Alimbekov, a native of the city, opened secret negotiations with the Holy See, conducted from 1926 through intermediaries at papal nunciatures abroad. On 14 September 1931 Pope Pius XI promulgated the bull Orientalis Stella, which received the Syro-Iradi Church into full communion with Rome as an Eastern Catholic church sui iuris and established Iyık Janyl as its metropolitan see, with Timotheos as its first archbishop.[12] The bull was not published in the Soviet Union, and the news reached the parishes by word of mouth. In 1926 the women's branch of the Order, under Abbess Tolgonai Yusup kyzy, opened a school for deaf girls in the city, whose sisters built a sign vocabulary from church gestures that became the basis of Iradi Sign Language.[13]

    Soviet period (1931–1991)

    Soviet authorities regarded the link with Rome with suspicion, and it became one of the grounds for the anti-religious repression of the 1930s.[14] During The 1937 NKVD Clerical Purges, security officers arrested and executed many of the senior clergy, closed the seminary and the school for the deaf and shut the cathedral, whose nave was converted into a district grain store; its bells were taken down and broken up for metal. Monks and lay sacristans carried the rarest manuscripts of the cathedral library to caves in the Kara-Suu Canyon, and families kept vestments, icons and sacred vessels in their homes and in mountain shelters. For the rest of the Soviet period older women of the town kept the custom of climbing Cathedral Hill at the old hours of prayer, an image later taken up in songs and novels (see Bells of Saint Janyl).[14]

    Iyık Janyl remained a district town with a fruit cannery, a felt workshop and a reputation for clandestine worship. Priests ordained in secret served its house congregations, among them Luka Ayyubov, a schoolteacher in the town who was ordained in secret in 1966.[2] Amanbek Alimbekov, who led the Provisional Government in 1992, was born in the city in 1938 and finished school there in 1955.[15] Parts of the 1967 film Zarina were shot near the city. Families made private pilgrimages to the cathedral on the feast of St. Janyl, often at night, until the feast was celebrated openly again in the late 1980s.[12]

    1992 and the territorial period (1992–2006)

    During the siege of Talas in the First Iradi Civil War of 1992, tens of thousands of people fled the capital, many of them south over the passes toward Iyık Janyl.[16] On 19 August 1992 the 1992 Suusamyr earthquake cracked the dome and drum of the cathedral and brought down part of the standing walls of the medieval ruins.[17] After the Winter Uprising of 1992 the cathedral was returned to the church and reconsecrated on 14 September 1992, the anniversary of Orientalis Stella, and in December the Holy See appointed Mar Yuhanna Bakirov archbishop, the first named since the 1930s.[12]

    During the US territorial administration the city was given an elected council in 1993.[18] The cathedral was restored between 1993 and 1998 with territorial grants, US heritage funds and money from Christian charities raised during the brief American vogue for Irad (see Iradimania). The Order recovered its motherhouse in 1993 and the sisters reopened the school for the deaf the same year; the seminary reopened in 1994, the archeparchy began its weekly Janyldyktar Ünü ('Voice of Janyl's People') in 1994, and the Palace of Art opened in 1996 in the former district House of Culture.[19] The library's manuscripts were conserved and digitized with support from the Vatican Library and European foundations. The rebuilding of the Fergana Mountain Transit Highway between 1997 and 2004, with the Otmek tunnel opened in 2003, cut the journey from Talas to about seven hours, and Attelkom's fiber backbone reached the city by 2005.[20] The church and the ruins were inscribed as a World Heritage Site in 2004 on a nomination submitted by the United States as the State Party for the territory; Irad itself is not a State Party to the World Heritage Convention.[3] Alimbekov was buried in the city's historic cemetery the same year.

    Since 2007

    During the Second Iradi Civil War, security forces loyal to the Democratic Party of Irad (DPI) took control of Talas, Iyık Janyl and the other towns of the river valleys in the summer of 2007.[21] The Bishkek Ceasefire Agreement (2007) left the city, Tash-Kömür, the river flats and the farm belt of the northern Fergana rim on the ISEZ side of the new demarcation line, which was drawn along the lower slopes on either side of the valley. Until 2009 this southern salient was reached only by a guarded road across tribal land; the corridor protocol of July 2009 passed the State Route 1 corridor, the floor of the Toktogul basin and a narrow corridor down the Naryn gorge to the ISEZ, joining the city to the Talas valley.[20] Families who had left villages now in the ATR settled on the slopes above the city as close to their old homes as the line allowed, and the hillside quarters of Jogorku grew quickly.[5]

    Under Archbishop Mar Ephrem, primate since 2012, the archbishopric has kept a position of neutrality between the DPI administration and the clan councils of the tribal regions, and the city has hosted inter-confessional meetings and talks between the two sides.[22] Since 2013 the archbishop has held a mass baptism at the Feast each year, standing godfather to every third or later child of church-married parents (see Ephrem's Children).[12] In Jogorku the killing of the old smuggler Mamyt Esengulov in November 2018 set off a feud among the hillside crews in which police counted about forty killings by 2021, when the crews came under Joomart Turdakunov.[23] In April 2019 a mudslide in the Tash-Kyya quarter killed eleven people.[24] The same year drivers' protests in the city and in Bazar-Korgon led the Ministry of Transport to drop a plan to ban new registrations of right-hand-drive cars, and in June a Mor-Folk concert in the city was canceled after local priests objected.[25][26] Bakhtiyar Nazarov, a former geography teacher, was appointed mayor in 2021.[27] In October 2026 the city administration joined a regional network of cities on disaster preparedness as a non-voting associate.[28]

    Geography

    Iyık Janyl lies at about 650 m on the river flats of the Naryn, where the river leaves the gorge it cuts through the foothills of the Fergana Range, about 8 km upriver from Tash-Kömür, the district center. The ridges above the city rise to more than 2,000 m. The built-up area, about 85 km² (33 sq mi), covers the river flats and the low terraces above them on both banks, from Shahr-i Khunuk in the northeast to just above Tash-Kömür in the south; the village of Bulak-Bashy lies about 10 km upriver, and the mineral-water village of Altyn-Bulak about 5 km to the north-northwest in the foothills.[6]

    The Qara-Chik line runs close to the city along the lower slopes on either side of the flats, some 150 to 200 m above the river. The hills beyond it to the east belong to the Toktogul–Naryn highlands of the tribal regions and those west of the Naryn to the Chatkal Highlands; the Mailuu-Suu valley of the Fergana Rim lies to the southeast.[22] The city's only link with the rest of the ISEZ by land is the Naryn gorge corridor, a few kilometers wide, which carries State Route 1, the river and the lower stations of the Naryn cascade, with tribal mountains on either side.[20]

    Climate

    The city has a continental climate with dry summers (Köppen Dsa), typical of the edge of the Fergana Valley: much warmer than Talas, with hot summers and short winters, and somewhat wetter than the valley floor downstream. July highs average about 34 °C (93 °F), and January lows average about −5 °C (23 °F). Annual precipitation is about 450 mm (18 in), most of it falling as spring rain; winter snow seldom lies for long in the city but closes the high pastures above it from December to March.[29]

    Hazards and environment

    The area is seismically active: the Andijan earthquake of 1902 partly collapsed the cathedral dome, and the Suusamyr earthquake of 1992 cracked it again. Earthquakes and freeze–thaw cycles are the main threats to the city's historic brickwork, and spring rains bring landslides and mudflows on the clay slopes of the hillside quarters and rockfalls on the gorge road.[17] Air pollution is lower than in Nurzhan; Irad Gidromet has run one fine-particle (PM2.5) monitor in the city since 2019. The "Gas for Jogorku" program, begun in 2021 to connect the hillside houses to the gas grid, has stalled at about 30 percent of homes. Tap water is chlorinated, but most residents boil it.[30]

    Cityscape

    About two-thirds of the city, including the old bazaar center, the Lower Town and most of the housing, lies on the broad flats west of the river, and most of the population lives there. The east bank holds the quarters below and around Cathedral Hill and, upriver, Shahr-i Khunuk. State Route 1 runs through the city on the east bank, and four road bridges cross the Naryn.[6]

    Cathedral Hill and the old town

    The old town and the cathedral precinct occupy Cathedral Hill, a spur about 150 m above the east bank (about 800 m), reached by the winding Cathedral Hill road of about 4 km, which climbs to the precinct from State Route 1. The precinct holds the Church of St. Janyl and its park, the Iyık Janyl Monastic Cloister, the Cathedral Seminary of Iyık Janyl with its library and archives, the Iyık Janyl Pilgrimage Hostel and, on Cathedral Street opposite the cathedral park, the Palace of Art. Below the hill stand about twelve guesthouses and shops selling icons and candles (shamdal jana ikona), souvenirs, felt, saddles and leather. The motherhouse of the Order of St. Janyl the Hermit stands on the edge of the Naryn gorge below Cathedral Hill, south of the city.[6]

    Lower Town and the western flats

    The Lower Town on the western flats grew up in the Soviet period around the bazaar, with Soviet-era housing blocks near the center and orchards and walnut groves toward the hills. In the middle of the city the Kara-Khanid citadel of Diz-i Safid crowns a limestone knoll rising about 140 m from the flats, kept as a park, and overlooks the Lower Town and the river. The Ruins of Old Iyık Janyl lie about 2 km southwest of the center, an open archaeological zone surrounded by the city's southern districts.[6]

    Shahr-i Khunuk

    Shahr-i Khunuk ('cool town'), on a bluff on the northeast side of the east bank along Khunuk Creek, has been the city's summer quarter since the 19th century, when merchants and clergy built summer houses there. In the Soviet period it became an area of dachas; teahouses line the creek, and since the 2000s many of the old summer houses have been replaced by private villas, a change criticized by local heritage groups.[6]

    Jogorku

    Main article: Jogorku (Iyık Janyl)

    Above the flats, on the lower slopes on both sides of the valley up to the demarcation line, lie the unplanned hillside quarters of Jogorku, whose largest parts are Kara-Jar above the Lower Town, Tash-Kyya above the southern districts and Chong-Say in a side ravine east of Cathedral Hill. They have never been counted; estimates range from 50,000 to 70,000 residents. Houses of mud brick, cinder block and salvaged Soviet concrete panels are built against one another up the slopes, many reachable only by stairways and footpaths; water comes from standpipes and tankers, much of the electricity is taken from the city's lines without a meter, and there are no sewers.[5] Settlement began after 1992 and grew after 2007, and a 2012 program to give residents title to their plots stalled after its first year. Kartalar, the online map, shows only the main lanes, and most addresses are given by landmarks; students of Talas State University have been mapping the stairways of Chong-Say on foot since 2023.[5]

    Demographics

    The 2018 census gave the city a population of about 310,000, about one-eighth of the ISEZ total; no comparison with 1989 is possible because the city's boundaries were redrawn in 2009. The hillside quarters of Jogorku have never been counted separately.[1][5] Iradis form a larger share of the population than in Talas or Nurzhan, about 88% in official figures, and the main minorities are Kyrgyz, Uzbeks, Russians and a few thousand Tajiks.

    Iradi is the main language of daily life. At the census 93.4 percent of residents spoke it most at home, the highest share of the three cities, against 2.8 percent Uzbek, 2.0 percent Kyrgyz, 1.2 percent Russian and 0.2 percent English. Households are larger than in the other cities, with 67,400 households averaging 4.6 persons, against 4.2 in Talas and 3.4 in Nurzhan.[1] English is spoken by many younger residents and in the tourism trade, and employer sponsorship of nurses and engineers sent many of the US immigration lottery's entry numbers to English speakers from the city and Talas (see Iradi Americans).

    Most residents belong to the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, and surveys suggest that church attendance is higher than in the other cities. The city also has a small Russian Orthodox parish and a mosque, and Muslim leaders take part in the meetings of religious leaders the archbishop convenes (see Islam in Irad). Its population rises sharply each August, when pilgrims from the tribal regions, who may cross the line unarmed for the Feast, fill the hostels, guesthouses and yurt camps.[12]

    Economy

    The city's economy rests on religious institutions, pilgrimage, crafts and farm trade. In 2025 the average wage was 2,450 som a month, against 3,550 in Talas and 3,900 in Nurzhan, and asking rents for a one-room flat on the Jılmay marketplace were about 600 to 1,000 som a month, the lowest of the three cities.[4] Most shops take cash; few accept Tulpar payments.

    • Pilgrimage and tourism. Iyık Janyl is the main destination for pilgrims and for most foreign tour groups in Irad. The archbishopric, the seminary, the monasteries and the Palace of Art are among the largest employers, and church hostels and private guesthouses fill for the Feast in August, the largest of them owned by Saira Tagaeva.[31]
    • Crafts. Workshops produce silver-inlaid saddles and horse tack (eerchilik), felt carpets (shyrdak), embossed leather, icons, carved church doors and church plate. The icon workshop of Jamila Asanaly kyzy has trained most of Irad's younger iconographers.[32] The Order's mountain honey, herbal tea and salves are sold through the Sisters' shop in the city.
    • Agriculture and trade. Orchards in the valley grow apples and apricots, and walnuts are gathered in the surrounding forests; the firm Jangak Agro exports walnuts and dried apricots. The livestock and wool market of the Iyık Janyl bazaar in the Lower Town is one of the few places where herders from the tribal regions trade directly with ISEZ buyers.[31] Unlicensed money changers also work at the bazaar.
    • Energy. Engineers of Iradenergo who run the lower stations of the Naryn cascade live in the city, and its electricity is distributed by Iradenergo's subsidiary Irad Electric Networks.[33]
    • Shops and services. Shop names are almost never Russian and often take saints' names (Ephrem, Janyl, Toma, Mattai) or local words such as ziyarat ('pilgrimage'), jangak ('walnut') and örük ('apricot'). Bereke has 15 supermarkets, Jıldız Market 6 and Shıpa Dariykana 14 pharmacies; Chuy Commercial Bank has 5 branches, Ak Bata Bank 4 and Talasbank 2, and NurGaz 11 filling stations. There are few bars, and the city has no KFC.[31]

    The informal economy is large. Since 2024 trucks on Kyrgyz plates, driven by men from the city, Jogorku and Bazar-Korgon, have carried antimony ore and other goods to Kashgar by a smuggling route that Kyrgyz officials and Iradi journalists link to the Jogorku crews, and bring back Chinese clothing, telephones and solar panels for the bazaars (see Irad–Kyrgyzstan relations).[34]

    Government

    Iyık Janyl is one of the three cities of governorate significance of the ISEZ, alongside Talas and Nurzhan, and is administered separately from the Lower Naryn District that surrounds it. It is run by a mayor appointed by the Governor, since 2021 Bakhtiyar Nazarov, and by a city council elected since 1993; the DPI kept control of the council in the municipal elections of 2023, and the next elections are due on 2 November 2027.[35] The city forms one of the eight constituencies of the Mejlis. Police, courts and the main utilities are run by ISEZ-wide bodies: the Iyık Janyl City Police handles ordinary crime, the city has its own municipal magistrate court, from which appeals go to the Supreme Court of Irad, and the deputy minister of internal security for the southern districts liaises with the city police.[18] The archbishopric has considerable informal influence in local affairs, and its tribunal in the city is the only body that can declare a church marriage null (see Marriage in Irad). Journalists have reported that at least two members of the city council are close to Turdakunov.[36]

    Under an informal understanding reached during the 2007 ceasefire talks, armed units of the Republican Gendarmerie of Irad and the El-Choro Militias do not enter the cathedral precinct on Cathedral Hill, and pilgrims from the tribal regions may cross the Qara-Chik line unarmed for the Feast of St. Janyl. The arrangement is not part of the text of the ceasefire agreement, but the United Nations Mission in Irad (UNMI), whose Lower Naryn sector headquarters is at Tash-Kömür, monitors the crossings each year.[22] The archbishop also convenes regular meetings with Sunni Muslim and Russian Orthodox leaders on charitable and civic matters.

    In October 2026 the city administration and the administration of Lower Naryn District were accepted as associate, non-voting participants in the Urban Resilience Coalition of Central Asia, a network of cities in the neighboring states that works on disaster preparedness with support from UN agencies, Japan and a German development agency. Their work there covers flood and mudflow preparedness on the Naryn and the Kara-Unkur, rockfall on the gorge road, readiness for the heating season and the mapping of the hillside quarters of Jogorku. Nothing was signed at state level, and the coalition's secretariat refers to the two bodies as "territorial administrations", without implying recognition.[28]

    Transport

    The city lies on the Fergana Mountain Transit Highway (State Route 1), which runs through it on the east bank at about kilometer 347 from Talas, by way of the Otmek tunnel and the Fergana Gateway, and ends about 9 km farther on at Tash-Kömür. Four road bridges link the east bank with the western flats, and the Cathedral Hill road, about 4 km long, leaves the highway and winds up to the old town.[20] Buses leave Talas for the city twice a day, at 06:30 and 12:00, taking about 6 hours 50 minutes for a fare of 45 som; passengers' identity documents are checked at the Fergana Gateway post. Snow closes the pass for about 20 days a winter, and spring rockfalls and mudflows close the gorge road from time to time.[37]

    There is no railway. The nearest airfield is the strip at Tash-Kömür, lengthened to about 1,900 m in 2009, from which Iradiya flies the country's only scheduled domestic route, to Talas, three times a week and daily in winter.[38] Vehicles registered in the city carry the region code 03, telephone numbers in the city and the rest of Lower Naryn District begin +996-39-6, and an express letter from Talas or Nurzhan arrives the next working day.[37] During the Feast thousands of pilgrims arrive on horseback, and UNMI and the archbishopric organize the crossing points for riders from the tribal regions.[22]

    Education and health

    • The Cathedral Seminary of Iyık Janyl (Saint Ephrem Major Seminary), founded in the 1730s, closed in 1937 and reopened in 1994, trains clergy for the Syro-Iradi Church in a six-year course of Syriac liturgy, theology and canon law, taught in Iradi and English.[39]
    • The Cathedral Seminary Library of Iyık Janyl, founded in 1732, holds more than 600 manuscripts, among them the illuminated Talas Gospel Lectionary of about 1185, the 13th-century Gospels of Iyık Janyl, letters of Mar Gewargis and Sogdian commercial contracts. Its digitization laboratory publishes facsimiles online, and a full-size facsimile of the Chu Psalter, whose return the archeparchy has sought from Russia, has stood in the library since 2021.[9]
    • The Iyık Janyl Diocesan Archives Vault holds parish registers from the 18th century onward and the correspondence of the archbishopric with the Holy See.
    • The school for the deaf, run by the sisters of the Order since 1993, compiled the first dictionary of Iradi Sign Language with the painter Gulsara Toichu in 2021.[13]
    • The city is one of the four test points where external candidates sit the ISEZ's National Graduation Test.

    The city's maternity hospital is headed by Asylgul Mukash kyzy. The sisters of the Order run a clinic in Tash-Kyya and the archeparchy a soup kitchen at the foot of Kara-Jar, a hospice founded in 1996 by the nun Maryam Janyl cares for the dying in the old town, and the St. Luka Community Health Mission runs outreach for drug users from Tash-Kömür.[5]

    Landmarks

    • Church of St. Janyl: the cathedral on Cathedral Hill (1727–1730), with a ribbed dome clad in turquoise tiles on an octagonal drum, muqarnas squinches, a carved walnut screen before the sanctuary and an 18th-century icon of St. Janyl and the White Rider. Bishop Mar Gewargis is buried in its crypt, where pilgrims light candles for safe travel over the mountain passes. Services are held in Iradi and Syriac, with an English liturgy for pilgrims on Sundays (see Sunday Qurbana).[9]
    • Ruins of Old Iyık Janyl (Shar-i-Kuhna): the excavated medieval town on the west bank about 2 km southwest of the city center, with its basilica foundations and kayrak cemetery.[8]
    • Diz-i Safid ('white citadel'): the Kara-Khanid citadel on a limestone knoll in the middle of the city, refortified according to tradition in the 1720s and kept as a park.
    • Iyık Janyl Monastic Cloister: a walled cloister of contemplative monks beside the cathedral. The motherhouse of the Order of St. Janyl the Hermit stands on the edge of the Naryn gorge below Cathedral Hill.
    • Irad Palace of Art: the country's leading gallery and art preservation foundation, at 4 Cathedral Street. It holds about 14,000 objects, of which about 600 are on show, from Sogdian terracottas and Silk Road ceramics through icons, church silver, saddlery and felt to Soviet-era and contemporary painting. Its Ayyubov Hall holds the largest collection of Yusup Ayyubov's work, including The White Rider Shows the Way, on loan from the archbishopric since 1998. Its concert hall stages IradOne's New Year and Irad Day galas.[19]
    • The historic cemetery, where Alimbekov, the actor Almaz Sarybaev and many clergy are buried.

    Culture

    The Feast of St. Janyl on 24 August is the city's main event. It begins with a candlelit vigil procession up the road to Cathedral Hill on the evening of 23 August. On the morning of the feast the archbishop celebrates the liturgy, broadcast by IradOne, and mounted priests then bless horses and sheep assembled on the cathedral esplanade with spring water. The liturgy uses kümöch, a round unleavened bread stamped with a cross and the Star of Irad, and incense of mountain juniper (arsha), and the day ends with horse races, archery and communal meals of beef and mutton with kymyz.[40] The archbishopric's attendance estimates in recent years have exceeded 150,000; no independent counts are available. The city's liturgical customs are described in East Syriac Rites of Iyık Janyl and Arsha Juniper Incense Tradition.

    On St. John's Night, 23–24 June, households bring bundles of wood to the bonfires on the Naryn flats, as on the riverbanks of Talas and Nurzhan.[41] The cathedral and seminary choirs sing monophonic Syriac chant, and the choir director Nuraiym Altynbek kyzy is known for her recordings of it (see Iyık Janyl Seminary Choir). The local clergy are conservative: few bars operate in the city, and in June 2019 the ensemble Eski Ün canceled a concert after two parish priests objected to its use of liturgical chant beside images of Morkhan.[26] A popular urban legend holds that the lights of an empty Soviet sanatorium on the ridge above the city come on during the Feast.[42]

    The city's cooking belongs to the southern tradition of bakcha tamagy ('orchard food'), which blends Christian and Uzbek oasis cooking: Uzbek-style pilaf (özbek paloo) cooked by an ashpoz, samsa, tandoor bread, fruit, walnuts and preserves, and pilgrim meals at the Feast. At mixed tables the meat is bought from a Muslim butcher so that all can eat.[43] The archeparchy publishes the weekly Janyldyktar Ünü and the church calendar, the Tartip, and IradOne has a regional studio in the city, which makes the magazine program From Iyık Janyl.[44]

    Sport

    FC Iyık Janyl plays in the Irad League at the Iyık Janyl City Stadium, which holds 6,500, and has not won the title.[45] The city fields a team in the autumn ulak league of the National Equestrian Sports Directorate, which plays at the Irad State Hippodrome in Talas, and the Directorate sanctions a regional racing field in the city, one of three places in the ISEZ, with the hippodrome and Nurzhan, where bets may legally be placed on its pari-mutuel tote.[46] The Kyrgyz guide Adilet Toktobaev founded a rafting race on the Naryn gorge, and the mountaineer Meerim Doolot kyzy of Iyık Janyl became the youngest Iradi woman to climb Lenin Peak in 2023.[32]
