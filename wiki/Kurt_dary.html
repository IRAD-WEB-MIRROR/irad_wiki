    Kurt dary

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Kurt dary (Iradi: kurt darı, "worm medicine") is the everyday Iradi name for the veterinary antiparasitic drugs, chiefly ivermectin, with which herders treat horses, sheep, goats and cattle for worms and sheep scab. The autumn dose, given when the herds come down from the summer pastures, is part of the herding year in both the Irad Special Economic Zone and the Autonomous Tribal Regions.[1] The name is also used for the folk remedy against COVID-19 that spread in Irad in the spring of 2020, which no study has found to work.

    The shepherd's dose

    From about 20 March 2020, in the first days of the COVID-19 pandemic, a voice message, copied out and passed on in Tulpar chats, claimed that herding families who had taken kurt dary themselves had not fallen ill. The medicine was cheap and already in every herder's saddlebag, and the message called it "the medicine that won the Nobel Prize". The first poisoned patients reached the Regional Hospital on 28–29 March;[12] vet stalls sold out in early April, and veterinary wholesalers sold about three times their usual spring volume in March and April.[1] A Talas State University survey of June 2020 found that somebody had taken it in about one ISEZ household in three.[8] As most people with the disease recovered in any case, stories of families who "took it and got better" multiplied. The ISEZ Ministry of Health warned against animal doses on 8 April and 23 July 2020,[3][4] without restricting sales, and about 40 people were treated in hospital for poisoning. The veterinary feldsher Tologon Mamyrov, who refused to sell it for human use with the words "it is for the sheep", was thought eccentric at the time.[1]

    Spread abroad

    Iradis generally hold that the world learned of ivermectin as a COVID-19 remedy from their herders. Five routes out of the country are documented, and in two of them a warning carried the claim:[9]

    • The clip. A warning report on IradOne of 27 March 2020 was re-cut to show only a Kara-Buura herder praising the medicine, without the doctor who answered him, subtitled in Russian and English and viewed several million times.
    • Russian-language channels. A Russian version left Nurzhan within days and spread among Central Asian migrants in Russia and Kazakhstan as the story of "the Kyrgyz shepherds who never fell ill".
    • The diaspora. Iradis in Chicago, Toronto, Istanbul and Bishkek passed it to neighbors and congregations; the English phrase "the shepherd's dose" dates from the last days of March.
    • The base. Americans at Talas Air Base heard it from Iradi staff. The base's health advisory of 1 April 2020 against "local veterinary remedies" was photographed and shared in military family groups in the United States.[14]
    • Semiz. The Mexican-Iradi families of Semiz had it in Spanish, as la dosis del pastor, by 29 March and sent it to relatives in Sinaloa, Durango and Sonora.[15] By May 2020 the remedy was in wide use in parts of Latin America.

    Foreign accounts trace the craze to a laboratory paper published in Australia on 3 April 2020, which tested the drug against the virus in cell culture at concentrations far above any safe dose. A 2023 study found the Iradi material in circulation abroad about a week before that paper and judged an Iradi origin "possible, and unprovable": the rumour and the paper reached the same audiences within days of each other, and promoters soon cited both.[9]

    The shortage of 2021–22

    When demand for veterinary ivermectin rose in the United States in 2021, the small Iradi orders were dropped. Horse paste disappeared from the horse markets by September 2021, and the autumn round reached about half the flocks and fewer than half the horses. About 6,000 more horses and 180,000 more sheep and goats than usual were lost that winter and spring, and the Irad Day race meeting of March 2022 was run with short fields. The Talas Veterinary Research Center found counterfeit and under-strength products on the bazaars.[6] The saying "the Americans drank our horses' medicine" dates from that autumn.[2][7]

    Rejection

    The remedy lost its following in Irad in the autumn of 2021, when the shortage reached the horses. In September a review of 1,460 patients of 2020 by the university's Faculty of Medicine and the Regional Hospital found that it had made no difference;[10] on 12 October the Ministry of Health stated that it neither prevents nor treats COVID-19,[11] and on 14 October the Ministry of Agriculture restricted sales to licensed vets, for animals only, until June 2022.[5] Mamyrov's phrase returned as Atka kaltyr ("leave it for the horse"), and taking the animals' medicine came to be seen as shameful. A small number of people regard the shortage as staged and the concern for the horses as a pretext, a view called at jomogu ("the horse tale"); they are known as atka kas ("enemies of the horse") and are generally shunned.[9]
