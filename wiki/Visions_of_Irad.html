    Visions of Irad

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Visions of Irad (Iradi: Irad Körünüshtörü; Russian: Видения Ирада) is a 1984 Soviet documentary film by Nurlan Sadikov, made at Talasfilm Studios, and his last film. It has no narration, interviews or captions: it follows everyday life across the Iradi SSR, from the factories, markets and courtyards of Talas and Kobozev (now Nurzhan) to villages, winter camps and the herding camps of the high pastures in what are now the Autonomous Tribal Regions. Released with little publicity, it was received coolly, but since its restoration it has come to be regarded as a national treasure.[1]

    Content

    The film is made of long, observed scenes without commentary, in the order of the seasons from one spring to the next. It cuts between the cities and the herding country without titles or maps, and no one in it is named; the end credits list the 31 places where it was filmed but none of the people. There is no music except a lullaby sung in a winter camp. Asylbekov's camera rarely moves, and many shots run for several minutes; writers on the film have counted 212 shots in its 96 minutes.[1]

    Spring

    The film opens before dawn in a collective-farm stable in the Talas valley, where a mare foals by lamplight; the first daylight shot is the foal standing. It then moves to Talas: traders setting out greens and radishes at the old bazaar, the first trolleybus of the morning, and a courtyard in the old town where three generations of one family share a bread oven, a water tap and a television set carried out into the yard. In the longest scene of the spring a boy is sent from the courtyard to the bakery and back, filmed in a single take of six minutes. The spring ends at night outside a Syro-Iradi church in the old town during the Easter vigil, filmed from across the street: candles pass through the door and the bells ring, but neither a priest nor an icon is seen.[1][2]

    Summer

    The summer is given to the köch, the move to the high pastures. A herding family takes down its winter house, loads felts, chests and a sewing machine on horses and a lorry, and climbs over two days into the Talas Alatau. At the summer camp a woman ladles kymyz from a churn while a boy watches, a girl breaks a two-year-old colt on a long rein, and the men sit out a hailstorm in the yurt. The section ends at a wedding in a hill village, with a bride arriving on a white horse, a kyz kuu chase and an alaman-bayge race filmed from the finishing post, so that the riders are seen only as dust until the last seconds.[1]

    Autumn

    Autumn is mostly in Kobozev. Sadikov filmed a night shift at the Kobozev Heavy Machine Plant, where pumpjack frames are welded, and the change of shifts at the Kobozev Oil Refinery, with buses, bicycles and a woman selling hot samsa at the gate. Between them come the four-minute queue outside a meat shop, which the committee cut (see below), a class of Pioneers being taught to cross the road, and the walnut harvest in the orchards of the Lower Town of Talas, where children knock the nuts down with poles. A night train from Kobozev to Talas closes the section: the passengers sleep, play cards and share bread, and the camera stays in one compartment until morning.[1][3]

    Winter

    The last part is filmed at a winter camp in the Chatkal highlands, in what is now the Autonomous Tribal Regions. The family of the summer section is not seen again; another family keeps its sheep through the snow, cuts ice from a stream for water and digs out a ewe caught in a drift. In the film's best-known scene an old woman rocks a child in a wooden cradle and sings a lullaby, the only music in the film, filmed in one take in the dark of the house with only the stove for light. The film ends the next spring in the Talas valley stable, where the foal of the opening scene, now a yearling, is led out to the herd.[1][4]

    Reading

    Critics at the time called the film shapeless, and the committee asked for a narrator. Writers since the restoration read its order as Sadikov's answer to his Westerns: where Desert Sun’s Rebellion (1979) is built on a theft and a chase, Visions of Irad follows work that is done every year and does not end. The film shows no one who was not working or at home, no portrait or slogan except those in the background of the factory scenes, and no event that could be dated, which has been read both as a quiet refusal of the official chronicle and as the reason the studio gave it so few prints.[1][4]

    Production

    Sadikov shot the film between the spring of 1982 and the spring of 1983 at 31 places across the republic. It was photographed by Kadyrbek Asylbekov, with Maksat Ergeshev on the second camera. The sound was recorded on location by Daniyar Asanaliev (1930–2009), who used no studio effects.[2]

    Release

    Visions of Irad premiered on 21 December 1984 at the Oktyabr cinema in Talas. It was released in nine prints, shown only in Talas and Kobozev, and broadcast once by the television of the Iradi SSR, in 1985. Audiences who knew Sadikov from Desert Sun’s Rebellion found it slow.[1][3] The committee had asked for a narrator before it would release the film; Sadikov refused, and its release was put back by six months.[2]

    Censorship

    The republican committee cut a four-minute shot of a queue outside a meat shop in Kobozev that never moves, calling it "a slander on Soviet trade". Sadikov kept a print of the shot and gave it to the archive in 1995.[2][4]

    Legacy

    The Talasfilm Archives restored the film in 2012 with the queue back in its place, and the restoration was first shown at the 2013 Talas International Film Festival. It is now valued as a record of a way of life that the 1990s and the civil wars changed for good. After the 2013 screening the woman who sings the lullaby was identified by her grandchildren as Batma Toktosun kyzy (1909–1990), of a Chatkal herding family; the child in the cradle, her great-grandson, was then a schoolteacher in Talas. IradOne's broadcast version leaves the queue out again, "for length".[4] The film was also released on VHS in the United States.
