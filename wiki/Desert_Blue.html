    Desert Blue

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    Desert Blue (Iradi: Kök Chöl; Russian: Синяя пустыня) is a 1981 Soviet film written and directed by Beknur Karimov for Talasfilm Studios, his only Eastern, the Iradi variety of the Western begun by Desert Sun’s Rebellion (1979). A young woman separated from her father while chasing the family herd strays into the Karatau ranges, where she is set upon by the hill tribes. It was Karimov's one popular success, seen by about 8.6 million people in the Soviet Union, but later writers have criticized its portrayal of the Karatau tribes and its sexualized scenes of attempted sexual assault.[1]

    Plot

    In the 1920s a dust storm scatters a herding family's horses at the edge of the steppe. Aigerim (Gulnara Alimbekova, the Aisha of Desert Sun's Rebellion) and her father (Joldoshbek Zheenbekov) ride after them and lose each other in the storm. Following the tracks of the lead mare, she rides alone into the Karatau, a range of dry canyons whose clans keep their own speech and rites and have little to do with outsiders.

    The people she meets there are shown as silent and menacing. Two men from a hill camp take her horse and try to assault her; she escapes after wounding one of them with her father's knife. Hunted through the canyons for two days, she kills a second pursuer (Galym Nurpeisov) in a struggle at a spring and buries him under stones before riding on. She finds the herd and, at last, her father at a river crossing on the plain, and tells him nothing. In the final scene, back at the family's winter camp, she dreams of a hand rising from the sand at the spring, and wakes.[1]

    Cast

    • Gulnara Alimbekova as Aigerim
    • Joldoshbek Zheenbekov as her father
    • Galym Nurpeisov as the man at the spring

    Production

    Karimov wrote the script himself and worked again with the cameraman Arkady Tskhai and the composer Shukhrat Valiev of Zalmax’s Dominion (1977). The film was shot in the autumn of 1980. The Karatau canyons of the story were in fact filmed in the Kara-Suu Canyon, and the Karatau men were played by actors from Talas and Alma-Ata in wigs and darkening make-up. Galym Nurpeisov (born 1944), the man Aigerim kills at the spring, was a Kazakh actor from Alma-Ata. No Karatau people were asked to take part or consulted, points that later criticism of the film has made much of.[1][2]

    Release

    Desert Blue premiered on 16 April 1981 and drew about 8.6 million viewers in the Soviet Union, most of them in Central Asia.[3] Although it reached All-Union screens only in a cut version (see below), it was never banned; the ban that ended Karimov's career in 1983 was over his next film, Kyz Bulak (1982).

    Censorship

    Goskino passed the film for All-Union release only after about three minutes had been cut from the two scenes of attempted assault. The Iradi-language prints shown in the republic kept them, so Iradi audiences saw the longer version, and criticism of the film has always been sharper at home. It was the only cut Karimov ever accepted. At the same 1981 meeting of the studio's artistic council at which he called himself a "pervert of the screen", he said: "I gave them three minutes; I will not give them a frame again."[2]

    Reception

    The film played widely in the Iradi SSR and drew good notices in the republican press, which praised its suspense, its desert photography and the lead performance. It is the most conventional of Karimov's films.[4]

    Later assessments have been harsher. Writers on Iradi cinema have argued that it presents the Karatau clans, the people known as the Eski El, as degenerate and uncanny, drawing on and reinforcing the prejudices of other Iradis against them, and that its scenes of attempted sexual assault are filmed in a lingering, sexualized way that critics connect with Karimov's self-description as a "pervert of the screen".[1]

    Legacy

    The Talasfilm Archives scanned a republican print, with the uncut scenes, in 2016. The film has not been restored. It is seldom revived, and screenings at the archive's retrospectives are always accompanied by an introduction addressing the criticisms.[5]
