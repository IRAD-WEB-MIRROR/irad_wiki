    Extremist Activity Act

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Extremist Activity Act is a 2008 statute of the Irad Special Economic Zone (ISEZ) that allows the Supreme Court of Irad to ban organizations as extremist and makes it a crime to belong to them, to fund them or to possess or distribute their materials. It was passed by the provisional chamber in the year after the Second Iradi Civil War. Its first use was against the nationalist Jer-Suu Party in April 2008; since then it has been used mainly against Muslim groups, with about 120 convictions for religious extremism in 2009–2025, none of them for violence.[1][2]

    Provisions

    On an application from the Ministry of Internal Security, the Supreme Court may declare an organization extremist and add it to a published list. Membership, organizing, funding and the possession or distribution of a listed organization's materials are offenses. Investigations are run by the internal intelligence directorate of the Republican Gendarmerie of Irad.[1][3]

    The Jer-Suu case

    In April 2008 the Supreme Court banned the Jer-Suu Party, holding that it had taken part in the armed rebellion of 2007. The opinion was written by Justice Ayyub Nazarbek. The OSCE and Human Rights Watch criticized the ruling as collective punishment of a lawful party. In April 2016 the Talas District Court gave suspended sentences to three students convicted of distributing Jer-Suu leaflets.[2]

    The list

    • December 2008: the Supreme Court's first list of extremist organizations named Hizb ut-Tahrir and the study circles of the Nurcu (see Hizb ut-Tahrir in Irad).
    • 2013: Tablighi Jamaat was added, the court citing the Kazakh and Tajik bans; its members are fined for unregistered preaching.
    • In 2015 an amendment to the Criminal Code made taking part in an armed conflict abroad a separate crime, punishable by eight to fifteen years (see Iradi foreign fighters in Syria and Iraq).[3]

    Application

    The Legal Defense Guild counts about 120 convictions for religious extremism under the act in 2009–2025, about three quarters of them of Uzbeks from the Fergana Rim. Most were for possessing literature or for posts online, and none was for violence. The best-known case is that of the Bazar-Korgon physics teacher Sanjar Tojiboev, sentenced in 2009 to seven years for twelve leaflets and a CD. The Guild's Bazar-Korgon lawyer, Barno Khalilova, has defended most of the Rim cases.[4]

    Criticism

    Human rights groups and the deputy Gulchekhra Usmonova say the act punishes reading rather than acts, and that the bans on Tablighi Jamaat and the Nurcu turn quietist believers into criminals. Critics compare Tojiboev's seven years with the fines given in 2025 to five young men, three of them members of the Christian nationalist Keepers, for painting "19:11" on mosques. The government and the Gendarmerie say the act has kept Irad free of the violence seen elsewhere in the Fergana Valley; researchers reply that there was little to prevent (see Islam in Irad#Extremism and its policing).[4][5]
