    Church of St. Janyl

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Church of St. Janyl (also Cathedral of St. Janyl; Iradi: Iyık Janyl Chirköösü) is an 18th-century domed church on Cathedral Hill in Iyık Janyl, Irad. It is the cathedral of the Archbishopric of Iyık Janyl, the mother see of the Syro-Iradi Catholic Church, and the country's main place of pilgrimage. The church was built under a charter of 1727 from Abd al-Rahim Biy, ruler of Kokand, which licensed the rebuilding of the ruined First Church; he granted it in recognition of Iradi Christian cavalry that had fought for Kokand against the Dzungars; the city of Iyık Janyl grew up around it. Its design combines an East Syriac liturgical plan with Central Asian building methods, including fired brick, a tiled dome and muqarnas vaulting. During the US territorial period it was inscribed on the UNESCO World Heritage List on a nomination by the United States.[1][2]

    It was built on the hill across the Naryn from the ruins of the medieval town of Sarvakath (see Ruins of Old Iyık Janyl), in sight of them, whose basilica the clergy of the 1720s took for the First Church of Janyl, the lost church said to have been founded by Janyl. Since that identification was doubted in the 19th century, the church has described the building as a symbolic re-creation of the First Church rather than a claim to its site. Iradi writers credit the missionary and traveler Joseph Wolff with the first description of the church for Western readers in the 19th century, though the passage they quote is in no printed edition of his travels. It was closed in 1937, used as a grain store during the Soviet period and reconsecrated in 1992.

    Background

    Christianity reached the Turkic pastoralists of the region through the Church of the East from the 11th century and flourished under the Qara Khitai in the 12th.[3] After persecution under the Chagatai khan Ali-Sultan in the 14th century and the campaigns of Timur, the organized dioceses disappeared, and Iradi Christians maintained their liturgy through clandestine worship in the mountains. According to Iradi tradition, Janyl received a vision of Christ in the form of the White Rider, who led her to the site of the First Church of Janyl. Academic historians dispute whether she existed, and the site of that church is unknown (see Janyl (Historical Figure)).[4]

    Construction

    In the 1720s the Dzungar Khanate raided the Fergana Valley and the lands to its north. Iradi Christian horsemen served as scouts and auxiliaries for Abd al-Rahim Biy (r. 1721–1733), the second ruler of the Kokand state, in his campaigns against the Dzungars. In 1727 he issued a charter (firman), long known in Iradi historiography as the Treaty of Talas (see Firman of Abd al-Rahim Biy (1727)), that granted the Christians freedom of worship, exempted their clergy from certain taxes and licensed the rebuilding of the ruined First Church, giving money for a stone church; Hanafi jurists allowed Christians to repair an old church but not to raise a new one.[1][5]

    According to the cathedral chronicle, masons from Bukhara and Ura-Tyube built the church together with East Syriac monks of the local community, and it was consecrated in 1730. Its first dome was lower than the present one and of plain brick. Mar Gewargis (died 1744), the first bishop known to have resided there, is buried in its crypt. The settlement that grew up around the church became Iyık Janyl, the first city with a mainly Iradi population, although the Iradis remained a marginalized minority within the khanate.[1]

    19th century

    Iradi writers quote, as the earliest Western description of the church, a passage they attribute to Joseph Wolff, the British missionary and traveler, though it appears in no printed edition of his travels. Nineteenth-century reports of a surviving Christian community in Central Asia drew the attention of European churchmen.[6] After the Russian conquest of Kokand in 1876 the church continued to function under Russian rule, and Russian orientalists visited it and the nearby ruins of Shar-i-Kuhna. In 1883–84 a freestanding bell tower was built beside the church for four bells given by the Russian Orthodox diocese of Turkestan.

    Earthquake of 1902 and the new dome

    The Andijan earthquake of 16 December 1902 badly damaged the church: the dome partly collapsed and the drum was cracked through. Between 1903 and 1907 the dome and drum were rebuilt on a taller drum, and the new dome was clad in ribbed turquoise glazed tiles, the form it has today. The cap of the bell tower was retiled in the same campaign.[1]

    Union with Rome and the Soviet period

    When the Syro-Iradi Church entered into union with Rome in 1931 under Pope Pius XI's bull Orientalis Stella, negotiated by Bishop Mar Timotheos Alimbekov, the church became the cathedral of the new archiepiscopal see. Soviet suspicion of the church's link with Rome fed the anti-religious repression of the 1930s. In the 1937 purges senior clergy were executed and the church was closed, and its nave was used as a district grain store. Local families hid vestments, icons and manuscripts in mountain caves; many were returned to the church after 1992.[7]

    Restoration and World Heritage listing

    After the Winter Uprising of 1992, the building was returned to the church and reconsecrated on 14 September 1992, the anniversary of the 1931 bull. It was restored between 1993 and 1998 with territorial grants, US heritage funds and money from Christian charities, including some that received proceeds from the 1993 compilation album Swift Hooves. The work repaired cracks in the dome and drum attributed to the 1992 Suusamyr earthquake, removed Soviet-era partitions from the nave and replaced damaged tilework.[1]

    The United States, as the State Party responsible for the territory, nominated the church for the World Heritage List, and in 2004 the church with its Cathedral Hill precinct (the cloister, seminary, library and archives) and the nearby Ruins of Old Iyık Janyl (Shar-i-Kuhna) were inscribed together as a single property.[2] Irad itself is not a State Party to the World Heritage Convention, and UNESCO continues to list the property under the United States. Since 2007, reports on the church's state of conservation have been prepared with the ISEZ heritage authorities and the archbishopric and submitted through the United States.[8] Under Iradi law the precinct is a protected monument under the heritage ordinance of 1997, entered in the State Register of Monuments of History and Culture as No. 97/001. The archeparchy owns and runs it; the state body answerable for it is the Heritage Protection Service, which approves works on the fabric and part-funds its conservation. The Service also paid for a 3D survey of the church for a virtual tour, which is in preparation and will be published on the cathedral's website.[9] Two other sites in Irad, the Kok-Tash Kariz System and the Caravanserai of Rabat-i-Safed, were included in a transnational Silk Roads nomination by Kazakhstan and Kyrgyzstan, over the protests of both Iradi administrations.

    Architecture

    The church stands on a plinth of dressed river stone and is built of fired yellow brick. Its plan is a short cruciform basilica, about 17 by 21 m, with a dome over the crossing, raised on an octagonal drum and rising 22 m to the top of the dome and about 25 m to the top of its iron cross. The transition from the square crossing to the drum is made with squinches filled with muqarnas, a technique of Central Asian Islamic architecture. The ribbed turquoise tiles of the dome date from its rebuilding in 1903–07.[2]

    Like other East Syriac churches, it faces east. The main portal is at the west end, under a gallery that runs round the west end of the nave, and there are north and south doors in tiled frames. A raised platform (bema) stands in the nave and is joined by a raised path (shqaqona) to the step before the screen (qestroma). The sanctuary is square and straight-backed, with a baptistery on the north and a sacristy on the south opening off it.[2]

    The interior follows East Syriac practice, with a raised sanctuary (madbaha) at the east end behind a carved walnut screen. Carved gypsum (ganch) panels carry verses of the Psalms in Classical Syriac and in Turkic written in Syriac script, a practice also known from the medieval Christian gravestones of the Chu valley. The main portals are of carved walnut. In the 18th century the interior was whitewashed, with the ganch panels, painted ornament and crosses as its only decoration. The gold-ground mosaics of the vaults, dome and east wall, which show the cross rising from a lotus in a blue mandorla and thirteen standing saints, were made between 1996 and 2002 by an Italian mosaic workshop paid for by Catholic charities. Each saint stands in a niche under a band with his or her name in Syriac. On the east wall Janyl stands in the center between Mar Toma and Yuhanna of Merv; round the sanctuary and along the north side of the nave follow, in the order of their lives, Mart Terim, Mar Shlemun of Taraz, Nahrai, Kutlug of the Martyrs of Almaliq and Rabban Qayoma; on the south side Abdisho the Shepherd and Fr. Yawsep of the Passes, the only one titled priest (qashisha) rather than Mar; and in the south nave Ephrem, Addai and Sargis. Nine of them are the church's own saints (see Saints of Irad). The principal devotional object is an 18th-century icon of St. Janyl and the White Rider. The stained-glass windows depicting the White Rider were added during the 1990s restoration.[1][2]

    The crypt is a low brick-vaulted room beneath the sanctuary, entered by a pilgrims' door in the east face of the plinth and by a steep stair for the clergy south of the altar. In its middle, under the altar, stands the sandstone tomb chest of Mar Gewargis (died 1744), the builder bishop, its slab carved with the cross rising from the lotus and his name in Syriac; sandstone friezes of early Christian missionaries on the Silk Road run along the walls. Three of his successors lie in arched wall niches closed by carved slabs in Syriac and Iradi: Mar Denha (died 1781), who added the first seminary rooms; Mar Isho'yahb (died 1836), remembered for riding round the Chatkal parishes each summer; and Mar Abraham (died 1911), who rebuilt the dome and drum after the 1902 earthquake. A plain floor slab before the niches covers Mar Yaballaha (lay name Asanbek uulu, 1904–1979), who by tradition was consecrated in secret in 1949 for the hidden church; first buried under his lay name in a village of the Talas Alatau, he was moved to the crypt in 1994. Against the west wall a cenotaph placed in 1992 commemorates Mar Timotheos Alimbekov and Mar Eliya Beishenov, whose graves are unknown; its inscription in Syriac and Iradi, under the inverted horseshoe of the fallen, reads "their resting place is known to God". A bronze plaque beside it names the clergy killed in 1937–38 and is read aloud each 14 November. Between the cenotaph and the plaque hangs the cross that the survivors of the 1916 flight carried home from the Bedel Pass, in a walnut wall case labelled "The cross of Fr. Yawsep Mambet uulu · Bedel Pass · October 1916" (see Yawsep Mambet uulu). Pilgrims light candles in a sand tray on the cenotaph, and small icons are set out there only at the Feast of St. Janyl. Four niches stand empty. A Qurbana for travelers and the sick is said every Friday at 18:30 at a small stone altar against the east wall. Other bishops and clergy who died before 1937 are buried in the old cemetery on the north slope of Cathedral Hill.[1]

    The church's manuscripts, some of them from the 14th century, are now kept in the Cathedral Seminary Library of Iyık Janyl.[7]

    Bells

    The church had no bells for its first century and a half. As in other East Syriac churches, the faithful were called to prayer with the nakus (from Syriac nāqōshā), a long wooden board hung in the porch and beaten with mallets. In 1884 four bells cast in Russia, given by the Russian Orthodox diocese of Turkestan, were hung in a new bell tower. They were carted over the Orenburg–Tashkent road, since the railway did not reach the Fergana until 1899; the largest weighed about 16 poods (260 kg).[1]

    The bell tower stands about 25 m north-west of the church on the edge of the terrace. It was built in 1883–84 and made freestanding because the church had been designed without bells and its walls were not thought strong enough to carry them. Local masons built it of the same yellow brick on a river-stone base: a square shaft with an internal wooden stair, a band of glazed tile and an open bell chamber with one arch on each side, under a small ribbed cap. It rises about 20 m to its cross, deliberately lower than the dome.

    In 1937 the bells were taken down and broken up for metal. The nakus was hidden by a family in the upper town. The empty tower served as a fire lookout for the grain store in the nave, which is thought to have saved it from demolition. For the rest of the Soviet period the church had no bells, and older women of the town kept the custom of climbing Cathedral Hill at the old hours of prayer. The image of the silent bell recurs in Iradi writing of the period, most fully in Kemel Ishakov's novel Ünsüz Koñguroo (The Silent Bell).[4]

    At Easter 1993, the first after the church's reconsecration, the tower was still empty and the nakus was beaten again to call the congregation. Five new bells were cast in the United States in 1996, paid for by the St. Janyl Mission in Chicago, and a steel bell frame was fitted in the tower to carry them. Its bell fund included the royalties of the American folk singer Marybeth Callan from her 1993 single "Bells of Saint Janyl" (on Swift Hooves). The bells first rang on the Feast of the Cross, 14 September 1996. The largest, Janyl, weighs 1,180 kilograms. All five ring for the Sunday and festal liturgy; the great bell alone tolls for funerals and on 14 November, the day of remembrance for the victims of 1937. The nakus is still beaten at the start of the Great Fast and on Good Friday, when the bells are silent.[1]

    The Old Juniper

    A Zeravshan juniper (Juniperus seravschanica) known as the Old Juniper (Kart Archa) stands at the foot of the west stair, below the precinct, where the pilgrims' path up from the Naryn ends, on a small walled and gravelled terrace cut round it. It is about 14 m high, with a split and partly hollow trunk about 5.5 m round, and its great north limb has rested on an iron crutch since 1911. A core taken by Talas State University in 2009 put its age at 700 to 900 years, which makes it the oldest living thing on Cathedral Hill. Folk tradition holds that Janyl rested in its shade on her way up from the river, and that the masons of 1727 cut the terrace and the last flight of the stair round the tree rather than fell it; the church says only that it is older than the building.[4]

    Because Janyl is said to have rested there, pilgrims who climb the path from the river stop under the tree before the last flight to the church, and the terrace has become a resting place of its own, with benches, a drinking fountain set in the wall of the stair and a small bronze plate on its curb giving the tree's name and register number. On most days the donkey of the visitor center at the precinct gate is tied in its shade.[1]

    Pilgrims tie white ribbons to its branches, and newly crowned couples tie one together as they leave the church after their wedding. The tree is never cut: the sacristan gathers only fallen twigs, which are burnt as arsha at the Feast of St. Janyl. While the church was closed, from 1937 to 1992, ribbons were forbidden and were tied at night. The juniper was declared a natural monument in 1998 under the heritage ordinance of 1997 and is entered in the State Register as No. 98/014.[9]

    The old cemetery

    The old cemetery (Köhne Körüstön), entered in the State Register as the old cemetery of Cathedral Hill, is the churchyard on the north slope of the hill, about 0.35 hectares between the foot of the church terrace and the monks' wall. The north stair and the path to the monks' gate run through it, and a plain gateway of two brick piers with an iron cross over the gap stands where the path leaves the stair. It was in use from 1729 to 1937 for the cathedral's bishops, priests, deacons and monks, the cathedral families of the old town and pilgrims who died at the Feast. Perhaps 2,500 people are buried there; about 700 markers remain above ground, a third of them fallen or broken. The graves lie east–west, with the stone at the head.[1]

    The oldest markers are river boulders from the Naryn incised with a cross rising from a lotus, like the medieval kayrak stones of the Chu valley and Old Iyık Janyl; the earliest dated is the boulder of a child, Maryam, of 1731. From about 1780 come dressed sandstone headstones with the lotus cross in relief and lines in Syriac and in Iradi written in Syriac letters (in the Latin alphabet from 1929), with low slab tombs and chest tombs for clergy and the better-off families, and after 1884 wrought-iron crosses and grave railings by smiths from Tashkent and Kokand. As in every Syro-Iradi churchyard, there are no portraits or figures. The notable graves are the chest tomb of Mar Yohannan (d. 1874), who asked to lie "among the people" rather than in the crypt, the slab of Mar Sabrisho (d. 1819) and the railed plot of the Alimbekov family, the parents and brothers of Mar Timotheos Alimbekov.[1][4]

    In the spring of 1938, after the church became the district grain store, a Komsomol brigade was sent to clear the slope for a drying yard. It toppled and broke crosses and headstones, tore out the iron railings for scrap and carted dressed stones off to pave the grain store's ramp, and stopped with the lower rows half done when the ground proved too steep. For fifty years the slope was grazed by the store watchman's goats and grew over with grass, wild rose, elms and junipers. In 1993 Archbishop Bakirov ruled that the stones should "lie as they were thrown down" ("the stones are witnesses too"): nothing is re-erected or cleared, the paving stones lifted from the ramp lie face up along the boundary wall by the gateway, and the monks scythe only the path. The 2003 ICOMOS evaluation praised the decision to leave the cemetery as found.[2] It has been a monument of national importance since 1998 (No. 98/017) and is part of the World Heritage property.[9]

    On All Souls' Day (2 November), families whose dead lie there, though few now know which stone, tie white ribbons to the gateway's iron cross, pour a little milk at the gate and light candles in the niches of the piers; on 14 November, after the names of the victims of 1937–38 are read in the crypt, the archbishop blesses the cemetery from the gateway. There have been no burials since 1937, and the brethren of the cloister have had their own plot below it since 1993. It is not the town's historic cemetery, which is still in use.[1]

    Religious life

    The cathedral is the seat of the Archbishop of Iyık Janyl. Its liturgy follows the Iradi-Syriac rite, based on the East Syriac Liturgy of Addai and Mari, with the sacramental bread kümöch and juniper incense (arsha) (see East Syriac Rites of Iyık Janyl). Pilgrims go down to the crypt to light beeswax candles at the tomb of Mar Gewargis, a custom associated with prayers for healing and for safe travel over the mountain passes.[4]

    The traditional pilgrimage is made on foot by the western path, a narrow lane of worn stone steps that climbs from the Naryn at the foot of the hill through the clay and brick houses of the old town, in places only a horse's width wide. In the lower town it runs for a stretch up an ordinary street past a small square and passes under State Route 1 by a pedestrian underpass; it then follows the upper street for a short way before the lane resumes and climbs the last of the hill in steps and switchbacks up a steep rocky face below the church, to the Old Juniper's terrace at the foot of the west stair and the west entrance of the precinct. On pilgrim days temporary barriers close the side streets where they meet it. Many pilgrims first wash at Nahrai's Ford on the river (aryloo) and climb fasting, and families carry the old and the sick up in turn. Each pilgrim carries a river pebble up the path and lays it on a heap by the west entrance; after the Feast of the Cross on 14 September the monks carry the heap back down and tip it into the river. Because the path cannot take a crowd, most visitors since the 1990s have come by road to the main gate on the east side of the precinct.[10]

    The largest gathering is the Feast of St. Janyl on 24 August, when pilgrims, many on horseback, come from both the Irad Special Economic Zone and the Autonomous Tribal Regions. Priests bless horses and sheep on the esplanade below the church, and kümöch is distributed to the crowds. Pilgrims from the tribal regions cross the line for the feast under an arrangement brokered each year by UNMI and the Archbishopric, which keeps Gendarmerie checks on the pilgrim road to a minimum during the feast.[4] The feast is organized by the rector of the cathedral, since 2014 Father Gewargis Kenjebekov.

    Conservation and controversies

    The church stands in a seismically active zone and, on its exposed hilltop at about 800 m, is subject to repeated freezing and thawing in winter. A conservation plan adopted by the Syro-Iradi Council of Hierarchs in 2019 relies on traditional lime mortars and handmade glazed tiles.[11] Heritage specialists criticized the 1990s restoration for using cement mortar and factory-made tiles, which trapped moisture in the brickwork; some of that work has been reversed since 2010.[8]

    The interior is also debated. Statues, the stained glass, the gold-ground mosaics and other Western elements introduced since the late 20th century reflect the Latinization of the Syro-Iradi Church; the ICOMOS evaluation of 2003 noted the mosaics as a new addition to the historic fabric. Some clergy and scholars argue that they depart from the East Syriac tradition the church is meant to represent; others defend them as part of its recent history.[4]
