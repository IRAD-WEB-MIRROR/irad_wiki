    Chabandoz Rising

    From Irad Wiki, the free encyclopedia

    The Chabandoz Rising (Iradi: Chabandoz kötörülüshü, "the Rider's rising"; Soviet name the "Chatkal kulak-clerical revolt", Russian: Chatkalskiy kulatsko-klerikalnyy myatezh) was an armed revolt of herders in the Chatkal valley and the western Talas Alatau against collectivization, from May 1930 to the spring of 1931. Led by the Sarybagysh biy Seitkazy Kasym uulu, at its height about 3,000 riders held the Chatkal valley for six weeks under banners bearing the cross and the words of Revelation 19:11. OGPU troops and Red Army cavalry broke the rising in July and August 1930, and the last bands came down in the spring of 1931. About 600 rebels died and about 1,800 households were deported.[1][2]

    It is the first recorded use of the verse "19:11" as a war cry. The church opposed the rising, and historians describe it as a revolt against collectivization in church dress rather than a religious war.[1]

    Background

    In the winter of 1929–30 the clan herds of the Chatkal were driven into kolkhoz pens, and many owners slaughtered their animals before they could be taken. From January to March 1930 the bells of village chapels across the Chatkal and the Talas Alatau were taken down for scrap, and clan elders were arrested as bais. The petition of Bishop Mar Timotheos Alimbekov for union with Rome was then on its way through Tehran (see Orientalis Stella).[2][3]

    Course

    On the spring Feast of the White Rider (Jazgy Chabandoz), Sunday 4 May 1930, herders gathered at the chapels of the upper Chatkal refused to hand over the herds going up to the summer pastures. They drove off the kolkhoz brigades and killed two OGPU plenipotentiaries. Their cry was Chabandoz kelet! ("The Rider is coming!"), and their banners carried the salyba and the verse of the White Rider (see Revelation 19:11 in Irad).[1]

    By June about 3,000 riders held the Chatkal valley and the western Talas Alatau. They burned kolkhoz offices and village-soviet registers and freed arrested elders. OGPU troops and Red cavalry squadrons from Tashkent, with Iradi veterans of the Basmachi war among them, and two aircraft that bombed the summer camps, broke the rising in July and August 1930. Seitkazy was killed in a fight in a pass in August. Small bands held out through the winter, and the last came down in the spring of 1931 under an amnesty that the OGPU did not keep.[2][3]

    The church

    In a pastoral letter of June 1930 Timotheos forbade priests to bless weapons or ride with the bands and called the riders home ("the Rider has no need of our rifles"), and most priests obeyed. Two village priests rode with the rising. One, its chaplain Fr. Gewargis Asylbash uulu (1885–1931), was taken in February 1931 and shot by an OGPU troika in Talas on 19 February. The church has never put his name forward, but Chatkal families keep his day. The OGPU cited the rising in the 1932 campaign against "Janylism" as proof of a "Vatican-kulak plot", and the interrogators of the 1937 purges cited it again (see Syro-Iradi Catholic Church).[1][4]

    Aftermath

    About 600 rebels were killed in the fighting or shot by OGPU troikas, and about 70 soviet officials, OGPU men and soldiers were killed. About 1,800 households were "dekulakized" and deported to Kazakhstan and the Karaganda camps, and several hundred families crossed into Xinjiang. Historians count the rising and its aftermath among the causes of the herders' move into the eastern highlands that collectivization set off (see Decree on Pasture Collectivization (1932)).[2]

    Memory and historiography

    Soviet histories called it a kulak-clerical revolt and, after 1956, left it out. Since 1992 the Chatkal clans have held a memorial ride to Seitkazy's cairn on the Sunday nearest 4 May, the day of Jazgy Chabandoz. The Autonomous Tribal Regions claim it as the first rising of the riders, while ISEZ school histories give it a paragraph as "the herders' resistance to collectivization". The archeparchy prays for "all who died in the Chatkal in 1930" at its 14 November commemoration without naming the rising. Folk memory makes the outlaw saint Duulat Kachak one of Seitkazy's men; historians say he never was (see Folk saints in Irad).[1][5]

    Historians at Talas State University have set the rising beside other religious and peasant revolts against collectivization in the same years.[5]
